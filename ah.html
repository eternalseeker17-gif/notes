1Framing: Division of History & Archaeology

  • History divides into Ancient (earliest times–7th c. AD) → Medieval (8th–18th c. AD, starts with Islamic invasion) → Modern (18th c. AD onward, starts with British Rule). James Mill (The History of British India, 1817) split Indian History into Hindu/Muslim/British.
  • Ancient History = Archaeology (no written evidence, via excavation) + History (deciphered written evidence). Archaeology → Pre-history (Stone Age) + Proto-history (Copper Age, Bronze Age). C.J. Thomson devised the Three Age System: Stone → Bronze → Iron.
  • Quaternary Stage of Life: current geological era, began 2.58 million years ago — splits into Pleistocene (Ice Age, Palaeolithic humans) and Holocene (After Ice Age).

2Human Evolution

Africa = "the Cradle of Humankind" (original homeland of humans). Unitary Origin Theory (more accepted): modern human evolved in Africa from Homo Erectus, then migrated worldwide. Parallel Origin Theory: evolved in parallel from dispersed Homo Erectus populations globally.

Evolution line: Australopithecus Africanus (50,00,000–30,00,000 BC) → Homo Erectus/Pithecanthropus (30,00,000–1,00,000 BC, earliest biped) → Homo Sapiens Neanderthal (1,00,000–40,000 BC, named after Germany's Neander valley) → Homo Sapien Sapien/Cro-Magnon (40,000–10,000 BC, latest ancestor of modern man; local names Cro-Magnon/Chancelade[France], Grimaldi[Italy]).
KEY SITEHathnora (Narmada river, Hoshangabad dist., MP): India's only Palaeolithic skull find (not a full skeleton) — a woman's skull, discovered 1982 by Arun Sonakia (Geological Survey of India) — India's earliest finding of human remains; classified Homo Erectus.

4Mesolithic Period (9,000 BC–4,000 BC)

  • Time period: Parmar 9,000–7,000 BC (SSC convention: 12,000–10,000 BC); Lucent 9,000–4,000 BC. Transitional phase Palaeolithic→Neolithic; warmer climate, more flora/fauna; people = hunters & gatherers/herders.
  • Earliest evidence of animal domestication: Bagor(Rajasthan, on the Kothari river, Bhilwara dist. — also called "Baghore" in Lucent, same site — largest microlithic site in India) and Adamgarh(MP — 25,000 microliths found, plus bones of wild/domestic animals).
  • Credit for Mesolithic discovery in India: John Evans, excavated Sakkar & Rohri (Sindh), 1866.
  • Burial: earliest evidence — Sarainahar Rai & Mahdaha(Pratapgarh dist., UP) — grave goods (foodstuff, tools) show belief in afterlife. Dog+human skeletons together at sites like Adamgarh (dog = oldest domesticated companion). (Hathnora's Palaeolithic find was only a skull — Mesolithic sites are the first to yield complete human skeletons.)
  • Implements = Microlith/Pigmy tools (1–8 cm): refined scraper/burin/borer + new lunate/trapeze/triangle/arrowhead shapes; raw material shifts to agate/jasper/chert/chalcedony.
  • Other sites: Langhnaj(Gujarat), Chopani Mand(UP, near Belan Valley — world's earliest clay-pottery-use evidence), Sanganakallu(Karnataka), Tuticorin(Tamil Nadu), Damdama & Sarainahar Rai(UP, mass burial sites).

5Neolithic Period (7,000 BC–1,000 BC)

Three chief traits: crop cultivation (agriculture), animal husbandry, fixed abode/habitat. Pottery invented here (cord-impressed pottery) — to store excess grain; potter's wheel invented ~4,000 BC. Implements: pecked/ground/polished stone — celt, hatchet, chisel, pestle, arrow-head, saw. No real metallurgy = "metalless culture"; where limited metal appears, that level is called "Chalco-lithic."

V. Gordon Childe (Man Makes Himself) named this shift the "Neolithic Revolution." Discovery credits: Dr. Primrose — Lingasur, Karnataka, 1842; H.P. Le Mesurier — Tons river valley, Allahabad dist. UP, 1860.

7Iron Age & Megalith Culture (South India)

World Iron Age began ~1,300 BC; India similarly. 3 Indian sub-ages (Lucent): 1,300–1,000 BC(minor regions — Pirak, Hallur) → 1,000–800 BC(full Gangetic region + parts of Karnataka) → 800–500 BC(major N.+S. India — Megalith Culture).

Iron use in Gangetic Valley began ~1,000 BC (extensive by 600 BC) → cleared dense forests → paddy/cotton/wheat cultivated at scale → enabled the "2nd urban revolution" (1st = Indus Valley), at the time of Mahatma Buddha → by the 6th c. BC, 16 Mahajanapadas existed in Northern India. Greek historian Herodotus notes Indian soldiers under Persian king Xerxes using iron-tipped arrows, 480 BC — literary evidence for iron's spread.

3 Iron-using Cultures: PGW(Painted Grey Ware, 1,000–600 BC, grey pottery, Upper Gangetic doab) → NBPW(Northern Black Polished Ware, 600–300 BC, N. plains to Deccan) → ★Megalith Culture(South India, 1,000 BC–100 AD).

5Society, religion & script

5.1 Religion

  • No temples unearthed — religion inferred from figurines/seals
  • Most common figurine: Mother Goddess (Matridevi/Shakti) · evidence of Yoni worship
  • Chief male deity: "Pashupati Mahadeva" (proto-Shiva) — seated yogic posture, surrounded by elephant, tiger, rhino, buffalo, with 2 deer at feet · Lingam (phallic) worship also present
  • Shiva-Shakti worship = oldest form of worship in India, traced here (humped bull compared to Nandi bull; a Trident/Trishul is inscribed on pottery shreds found at Chandigarh)
  • Zoolatry (animal worship, esp. humped bull) and tree worship (esp. peepal) in vogue
  • Fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal — indicate fire worship

5.2 Script & seals

  • Seals made of steatite ★ · most common animal motif: Unicorn (one-horned animal) ★ · humped bull also on cylindrical seals ★
  • Script: pictographic, "Indus Script," written boustrophedon (right-to-left, then left-to-right) · not alphabetical · undeciphered till date ★
  • Longest known inscription: about 26 signs ★
  • Theorised as Proto-Dravidian; majority of scholars believe makers of the civilization were Dravidian
  • Swastika symbol traced to Indus Civilization

5.3 Burial & weights

  • Inhumation (complete burial) was the most common method · burial of dogs with masters found at Ropar · pottery/ornaments placed with dead (belief: usable in afterlife) · copper mirrors buried with dead · precious things generally NOT buried with the dead
  • Weights made of "Chert" stone, uniform, from Sukkur/Rohri hills (Sindh) · lower denominations in binary system, higher in decimal system

5Early Vedic — polity & society

Polity

  • Basic unit: Kula (family) → Grama (village) → Vis (clan) → Jana (tribe/people) → Rashtra (country/kingdom)
  • Patriarchal; monarchy normal (hereditary, primogeniture) but non-monarchical/elective forms also existed
  • King = Rajan; chief officials: Purohita (priest — highest-ranking official, king's guide) · ★ Senani = army chief/"war hero" · Gramani = village head
  • Assemblies: ★ Sabha (council of elders/privileged — also acted as a court of justice), Samiti (general assembly of the people), Vidhatha (oldest, religious/tribal-distribution body), Gana. Women could attend both Sabha and Samiti in this period.
  • No standing army; foot-soldiers & charioteers; weapons of wood/stone/bone/metal, arrows tipped with metal/poisoned horn; a moving fort (Purcharishnu) and a wall-assaulting machine are both referenced in the Rig Veda
  • Yajamana = the householder who sponsored/performed a Yajna, usually at his own home

Society & economy

  • ★ Four varnas (Brahmana/Kshatriya/Vaishya/Shudra) — based on profession, NOT yet hereditary/rigid
  • Family: monogamous, patriarchal; child marriage NOT prevalent; widow remarriage allowed (Niyoga); property inherited by son
  • Cow (Aghanya — "not to be killed") = chief measure of wealth; a wealthy man = Gomat; cattle-raid/battle = Gavisthi
  • Economy pastoral-agricultural; barter-based, no coins; some trade but limited; craftsmen (carpenters/smiths/weavers) existed; surgery/herbal medicine known
  • Rig Veda hymn: "I am a singer, my father is a physician, my mother is a grinder of corn" — shows occupations weren't yet hereditary

6Early Vedic — religion

  • Nature worship (naturalistic polytheism); gods = personified natural forces; no idols, no temples; fire sacred as intermediary between man and god
  • ~33 gods, later grouped into 3 tiers: Terrestrial (Prithvi, Agni, Soma, Brihaspati, rivers) · Aerial/Intermediate (Indra, Rudra, Vayu, Parjanya) · Celestial (Dyaus, Surya [5 forms], Varuna, Aditi, Usha, Ashvin)
  • ★ Most important: Indra (Purandara "fort-destroyer", war/rain god — 250 hymns) > Agni (fire, man-god intermediary — 200 hymns) > Varuna (water, upholder of Rita/cosmic order)
  • Religious character = Henotheism: many gods worshipped, but each in turn treated as supreme while being invoked
  • Core ritual = Yajna (sacrifice: milk/ghee/grain/flesh/soma offered)
  • Female deities: Aditi (mother of gods), Usha (dawn), Sinivali (fertility), Raka (prosperity), Aranyani ★ (goddess of the forest), Sindhu ★ (river goddess)

  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad is the first text to state the doctrine of transmigration (Punarjanma/Samsara-chakra) and Karma.
  • ★ 12 Ratnins (Satapatha Brahmana royal council): Purohita (priest), Mahishi (queen), Yuvaraja (crown prince), Suta/Sarathi (herald/charioteer), Senani (general), Gramani (village head), Kshata (chamberlain), Sangrahitri (treasurer), Bhagadudha (tax collector), Akshavapa (courier), Palagala (king's friend), Govikarta (forest dept. head)

1Causes of origin (both religions)

  • Vedic religion had grown complex — degenerated into superstitions, dogmas, costly rituals/sacrifices; people wanted something simple, easy to follow, economical. Supremacy of Brahmans created social unrest; Kshatriyas (Mahavira & Buddha both were Kshatriyas) reacted against Brahmanical domination.
  • New agricultural economy in Eastern India + expansion of Punch-Marked coins boosted trade — Vaishyas grew wealthy and funded these new, peaceful, trade-friendly religions to improve their own social standing (orthodox Varna hierarchy ranked them low despite their wealth).
  • Both religions: rejected the infallibility/authority of the Vedas (heterodox/Nastika systems); centred on strict Ahimsa; linked status to conduct/karma rather than birth. Jainism believes in divine figures; early Buddhism is agnostic about a creator God. Period called the "Age of Buddha"/Second Urbanisation (6th–4th century BC).
  • ★ Trap fact: unlike Jainism and Buddhism (both of Indian origin), Zoroastrianism did NOT originate in India (it is of Persian/Iranian origin) — a frequent "which religion is NOT Indian-origin" distractor-elimination question.

2Jainism · the 24 Tirthankaras

  • 24 Tirthankaras ("ford-makers"); 1st Rishabhadeva/Adinatha, last Mahavira. Historicity of the first 22 is ambiguous — solid proof exists only for the last two: ★ Parshwanath(23rd) and Mahavira(24th). Only Rishabh(1st) and Arishtanemi(22nd) are named in the Rig Veda; Vishnu Purana/Bhagavat Purana call Rishabha an incarnation of Narayana.
  • ★ Parshwanath: a prince of Benares who became a hermit, died at Sammet-Shikar (Parasnath Hill, Giridih, Jharkhand). Gave the first four vows — Chaturyama Dharma: Ahimsa, Satya, Asteya, Aparigraha. Mahavira adopted all four and added a fifth, Brahmacharya.

4Doctrines of Jainism

Triratna (Three Gems) — aim of existence:
Samyak ShradhaRight Faith — belief in the Tirthankaras
Samyak JnanRight Knowledge — of the Jain creed
Samyak KarmaRight Conduct — the 5 vows
Panch Mahavrata (5 vows): Ahimsa, Satya, Asteya, Aparigraha, Brahmacharya (first 4 = Parshwanath's Chaturyama Dharma; 5th added by Mahavira).
Five types of Knowledge: Mati (sense-perception), Shruta (scriptural), Avadhi (clairvoyant), Manahparyaya (telepathic), Keval (omniscience).

Syadvada/Anekantavada — "theory of maybe": all judgements are relative/conditional (7 modes of prediction possible — Saptabhangi Nayavad); absolute affirmation and absolute negation both wrong. Also called the theory of plurality/many-sidedness.

Mahavira: rejected Vedic authority/rituals, did not believe in a God, believed in Karma & transmigration of the soul, stressed equality (Varna tied to karma, not birth).

9Doctrine of Buddhism

Four Noble Truths (Chatwari Arya Satyani):
  1. Life is full of sorrow (Dukha) — Sabbam Dukkam
  2. Sorrow has causes (Dukha Samudaya) — Pratitya Samutpada
  3. Sorrow can be stopped (Dukha Nirodha) — Nirvana
  4. The path to end sorrow — ★ Ashtangika Marga (Eightfold Path)
Pratitya Samutpada = Hetuvada (theory of cause) + Kshanabhanga Vada (theory of impermanence). Madhya Marga (Middle Path) — avoid both luxury and extreme asceticism.
Ashtangika Marga (8 limbs): right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

Triratna of Buddhism: Buddha (the enlightened), Dhamma (doctrine), Sangha (community of monks).

Disciples: Sariputta, Moggallana, Mahakasyapa, Upali, Ananda, Subhuti.

6Foreign Invasions I — Persian (Darius, 518 BC)

  • ★ Achaemenian ruler Darius I (Darayabahu) penetrated North-West India in 518 BC, annexing Punjab (west of the Indus) and Sindh — India became the 20th province (Kshatrapi) of Iran's 28, its most fertile, yielding 360 talents of gold in revenue. Indo-Iranian contact lasted ~200 years.
  • Effects: boosted Indo-Iranian trade; introduced Greeks to India's wealth (→ paved the way for Alexander); gave India the ★ Kharoshthi script (written right-to-left, like Arabic); Iranian influence visible on Mauryan sculpture (bell-shaped capitals) and in Ashoka's edicts' preamble/vocabulary.

7Foreign Invasions II — Alexander's Invasion (326 BC)

  • Alexander succeeded his father Philip to Macedonia's throne at age 20; destroyed the Iranian empire; conquered the Kabul valley and North-West frontier, reaching Ohind near Attock in 326 BC.
  • Taxila and Abhisara's rulers submitted; ★ Porus (Puru) refused. Alexander crossed the Jhelum by a trick and defeated Porus at the ★ Battle of Hydaspes/Vitasta (modern Jhelum), ★ 326 BC — but treated him generously afterward.
  • After victory at Sakala, Greek forces reached the ★ Beas — Alexander's soldiers refused to go further (fearing the huge Magadhan/Nanda army, §5 above), forcing his return. Marched back Sept 325 BC; died at Babylon, 323 BC, age 33. His empire then split among his generals into Macedonia, Babylonia & Egypt.
  • Effects: opened India-Europe land/sea routes; enabled Indo-Bactrian/Indo-Parthian states → later shaped Indian architecture (Gandhara school of sculpture), astronomy, coinage; opened Indian rulers' eyes to the need for a unified empire; gives historians the "first reliable date in early Indian history" for chronology.

  • Causes of decline (as attributed by historians): highly centralised administration (Romila Thapar) · Ashoka's pacific policy (H.C. Raychaudhuri) · Brahmanical reaction (H.P. Sastri) · partition of the empire · weak later rulers · economic pressure (D.D. Kosambi) · neglect of the North-West frontier.
  • ★ Last ruler Brihadratha was assassinated in 185 BC by his own commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the Shunga Dynasty.

3The Huns & decline

TRAPDon't confuse the two Hun rulers: Toramana and his son Mihirkula (the white Huns first poured into India via Afghanistan in 458 AD; Toramana/Mihirkula ruled 500–530 AD) — Mihirkula was a Shaivite and a persecutor of Buddhism.   • Reasons for decline: relentless Hun invasions weakened Gupta power and hit its economy (visible in Skandagupta's debased gold coinage); Yashodharman's rise further chipped at Gupta authority; provincial governors and feudatories broke away — political disintegration followed.

4Administration

  • Centralised control was weaker than under the Mauryas — Gupta administration was decentralised/feudal in character, run through a network of self-governing tribes and tributary kingdoms.
  • Hierarchy: Bhukti(province, headed by Uparika/Bhogapati) → Vishaya(district, headed by Vishayapati/Ayukta) → Vithi(headed by Nagarpati/Purapala) → village (Gramika).
  • King assisted by a council of ministers, Mantriparishad/Mantrimandalam (its existence implied by the Allahabad Pillar Inscription's mention of "Sabhyas"/members).
  • Key officials: ★ Sandhivigrahika — minister of peace & war (office first appears under Samudragupta, whose own amatya Harisena held it) · Mahadanda-nayaka — minister of justice · Kumaramatya — the chief recruiting cadre for high officials · Mahapratihari — chief usher of the royal palace · Dandapashika — chief of police.
  • Notable Bhuktis: Magadha, Tirabhukti/Teerbhukti (N. Bihar), Pundavardhana, E. & W. Malwa, Saurashtra. Bundelkhand region known as Jejabhukti.
  • City administration by a council (Paura) of local representatives — a shift from Mauryan-era state-appointed committees.
  • Village headmen grew more influential than before; the army was largely feudal in character (though the emperor kept a standing army too); civil and criminal law were clearly demarcated for the first time.
  • Land tax (Bhaga) = 1/6th of produce (varying 1/4 to 1/6 per Lucent); other levies: Bhoga(periodic supplies-in-kind), Bali(originally voluntary, became an oppressive tax), Uparikara(extra tax on all subjects), Vishti(forced labour), Senabhakta(feeding the army on the march).
  • Land classified into 5 types: Kshetra(cultivable), Khila(waste), Vastu(habitable), Charagah/Aprahata(pasture/forest) — Parmar's parallel list: Kshetra/Khila/Aprahata/Vasti/Sarah.
  • Excess of land grants in this era — ★ Agrahara grants (tax-free land gifted to Brahmins) and Devagrahara grants — included transfer of royal rights over salt/mines (a Mauryan-era state monopoly, now given away).

5Society & economy

  • Varna system modified by 3 forces: foreigners assimilated as Kshatriyas; tribal peoples absorbed into the Shudra varna via land grants; craft guilds turned into castes as trade/urban centres declined. Shudras' position actually improved somewhat — permitted to hear epics/Puranas and worship a new god, Krishna.
  • From the 3rd century, untouchability intensified — Katyayana (Gupta-era Smriti writer) first used the term "asprasya" for the untouchable.
  • Women's position declined further: polygamy common, early/pre-puberty marriage advocated, no property rights except Stridhana (jewellery/garments). ★ First recorded Sati: 510 AD, Eran (MP) — Bhanugupta's Eran Pillar Inscription.
  • Vaishnavism grew popular under royal patronage; idol worship became a standard Hindu feature from this period; deities paired with consorts (Lakshmi-Vishnu, Parvati-Shiva); this era also saw the rise of Vajrayana/Buddhist tantric cults.
  • Economy: state treated as land's exclusive owner (per Buddhagupta's Pahadpur Copper Plate); largest gold-coin output in ancient India but of lower purity than Kushana gold; long-distance/Roman trade declined after the 3rd century — shift toward SE Asian trade via Tamralipti (east) and Bharuch/Kalyan (west).

6Art, culture & literature

Architecture

  • 3 categories: Rock-cut caves (Ajanta & Ellora, Maharashtra; Bagh, MP) · Structural temples — Dasavatara Temple, Deogarh (oldest & best, Vishnu), Bhitargaon brick temple (Krishna, UP) · Stupas (Mirpur Khas, Dhamek-Sarnath, Ratnagiri).
  • Nagara/Shikhara style pioneered — the garbha griha (sanctum) begins with the Guptas.
  • Udayagiri Varaha (Great Boar, Vishnu avatar) — most impressive Brahmanical image of the age; built under Chandragupta II. Sarnath seated Buddha — among the finest Buddha images. Ajanta frescoes — masterpieces of Gupta-age painting.

Literature

  • Kalidasa (Navratna): Abhijnana Shakuntalam (translated to English by William Jones), Meghadutam, Raghuvamsam, Kumarasambhavam, Malvikagnimitra, Vikramorvashiyam, Ritusamhara (his first poetry work) — known as "Shakespeare of India".
  • Sudraka's Mrichchhakatika ("the little clay cart") — love story of Charudatta & courtesan Vasantasena, realistic depiction of city life.
  • Scientific: Aryabhatiya/Surya Siddhanta (Aryabhatta — first to conceptualise zero) · Brahmasiddhanta (Brahmagupta, later translated to Arabic as "Sind Hind") · Panchatantra (Vishnu Sharma) · Kamasutra (Vatsyayana — earliest book on sex).
  • Manusmriti translated to English as "Institutes of Hindoo Law" by William Jones.

7"Golden Age" — reality or myth?

For:
  • Foreign rule removed; peace & prosperity
  • Enlightened, light-tax government
  • Hindu revival with religious tolerance
  • Sanskrit, art & literature flourished
  • Kalidasa, Aryabhatta, Varahamihira etc. all lived in this era
Against:
  • Too many feudatories; weak central army/bureaucracy
  • Rising feudal elements (land grants, serfdom, sub-infeudation)
  • Decline of trade, guilds & urban centres
  • Sharpening varna distinctions & social disorder
  • Declining status of women (first Sati, no property rights)

8Contemporary dynasty: the Vakatakas

  • Held sway over the Deccan/Central India between the Satavahanas' fall and the Chalukyas' rise. Founder Vindhyashakti → son Pravarasena I (real founder of the empire, performed 4 Ashwamedha Yajnas, ★ only Vakataka ruler titled "Samrat"). Feudatories of the Satavahanas; contemporaries of the Guptas; followed Brahmanism but patronised Buddhism too.
  • Tied to the Guptas by marriage: Chandragupta II's daughter Prabhavatigupta married Vakataka king Rudrasena II; after his early death she ruled as regent for her minor sons.
  • ★ Ajanta Caves (Sambhajinagar/Aurangabad, Maharashtra): 29 caves (4 Chaitya + 25 Vihara), built 2nd BC–6th AD under both the Satavahanas and Vakatakas (patron: Harisena — a different Harisena from Samudragupta's poet, a Vakataka king); UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1983.

1Pushyabhuti/Vardhana Dynasty

606–647 ADHarshavardhana
  • Founded by Pushyabhuti, ruling at Thaneswar/Thanesar (Kurukshetra dist., Haryana) — feudatories of the Guptas who broke free after the Hun invasions. First important ruler: Prabhakaravardhana (580–605 AD) — sons Rajyavardhana (eldest) & Harshavardhana (youngest), daughter Rajyashri (married Grahavarman of the Maukhari dynasty, Kannauj).
  • Grahavarman was killed by Devagupta (Malwa) & Shashanka (Gauda/N.W. Bengal), who occupied Kannauj and imprisoned Rajyashri. Rajyavardhana killed Devagupta but was himself killed by Shashanka (606 AD, by treachery). Rajyashri fled into the Vindhyan forests.
  • ★ Harshavardhana ("Siladitya") ascended 606 AD → Harsha Era begins. Rescued Rajyashri, drove Shashanka from Kannauj, and made Kannauj his new capital — the most powerful North Indian kingdom, extending to the Ganges Valley/Assam. Avenged his brother against Shashanka fully only after Shashanka's death (637 AD), conquering Magadha.
  • ★ Defeated by Pulakeshin II (Chalukya) at the Battle of Narmada, 618 AD — his only defeat. The Aihole inscription (Ravikirti) records the Chalukyas calling Harsha "Sakalottarapathesvara" (lord of the whole North).
  • Vallabhi conquest: defeated Maitraka ruler Dhruvasena II, then reinstated him as a feudatory and gave him his own daughter in marriage (Navsari copper plate). Sent an envoy to Tang Emperor Tai-Tsung (641 AD); received 3 Chinese missions.
  • ★ Hiuen-Tsang/Xuang-Zang, Chinese pilgrim, spent ~8 years (635–643 AD) in Harsha's dominions; wrote "Si-yu-ki" ("Records of the Western World"). Recorded the Kannauj Assembly (643 AD) — popularising Mahayana Buddhism — and the Prayaga Assembly (643–644 AD) at the Ganga-Yamuna-Saraswati confluence, celebrated every 5 years — per Parmar, the first historical evidence of the Kumbh Mela.
  • A Shaiva by faith but liberal toward all sects (esp. Mahayana Buddhism per Hiuen-Tsang). Nalanda University was funded by revenue from 100 villages he granted. Died 647 AD with no heir; usurped by minister Arunashva, who could not found a lasting dynasty.
  • An accomplished author himself — 3 Sanskrit plays: Nagananda, Ratnavali, Priyadarsika. Court scholar ★ Banabhatta wrote "Harshacharita" (biography of Harsha) and "Kadambari"; Mayur wrote "Mayur Shataka"/"Surya Shatak". Epigraphic sources: Banskhera Inscription (628 CE, signed by Harsha, found Shahjahanpur U.P.), Madhuban & Sonpat copper plates.
  • Contemporary dynasties (Parmar): Ikshvaku Dynasty (founder Chintamula I, capital Vijayapuri/Nagarjunkonda, Krishna-Guntur valley) and Vishnukundin Dynasty (capital Amarapuram/Amravati). Maukhari ruler Ishanvarman took the title "Maharajadhiraja."

2Chalukyas of Badami/Vatapi (543–755 AD)

  • Rose after the fall of Vakataka power; capital Vatapi/Badami (Bagalkot dist., Karnataka); earlier feudatories of the Kadambas of Banavasi.
  • Founder: Lucent — Jayasimha; Parmar — Pulakeshin I (543–566 AD). Pulakeshin I's sons Kirtivarman I & Mangalesha; Mangalesha killed by Kirtivarman I's son Pulakeshin II.
  • ★ Pulakeshin II (609/610–642 AD), greatest ruler — checked Harsha's Deccan ambitions, defeating him at the Battle of Narmada (618 AD), taking the title "Dakshinapatheshwara" (also Satyashraya, Bhattaraka, Maharajadhiraja, Parameshwara, Prithvivallabha). Exchanged embassies with Persian king Khusrau II (625 AD); hosted Hiuen-Tsang.
  • Defeated Pallava Mahendravarman I at the Battle of Pullalur, but was in turn defeated at the ★ Battle of Vatapi (642 CE) by Mahendravarman I's son Narasimhavarman I, who took the title "Vatapikonda" and sacked Vatapi. The Aihole/Pillar inscription about Pulakeshin II was composed by his court poet ★ Ravikirti.
  • Succession: Vikramaditya I (3rd son) recovered Vatapi from the Pallavas → Vinayaditya → Vijayaditya → Vikramaditya II (attacked Pallavas thrice) → Kirtivarman II (last ruler), defeated by ★ Dantidurga, founder of the Rashtrakutas (757 AD).
  • Chalukyan Architecture — 3 groups: Aihole ("Laboratory of Indian Temple Architecture," ~70 temples — Jinendra/Meguti temple by Ravikirti, Ladh Khan, Durga temple in Gaja-Prishta/elliptical form); Badami (4 rock-cut caves, Shaiva+Vaishnava+Jain side by side); Pattadakal (Nagara+Dravida blend, 10 temples — UNESCO 1987, "Cradle of Indian Architecture"; Virupaksha temple by Queen Lokamahadevi, Mallikarjuna temple by Trilokadevi).

3Pallava Dynasty (575–897 AD)

  • Origins debated — possibly a local tribe ruling the Tondaimandalam region, earlier feudatories of the Kalabhras; orthodox Brahmanical Hindus; capital ★ Kanchi/Kanchipuram. Founder: Parmar — Simha Vishnu; Lucent — Simhavarman. Contested the Krishna-Tungabhadra doab with the Chalukyas.
  • Greatest early ruler: Mahendravarman I (600–630 AD) — rock-cut architecture patron; Jain-turned-Shaivite; defeated by Pulakeshin II at Pullalur.
  • ★ Narasimhavarman I "Mahamalla" (630–668 AD): invaded the Chalukyas, killed Pulakeshin II (per Lucent) and captured Vatapi (~642 AD) → title "Vatapikonda." Began monolithic rock-cut temples; founded the port Mahabalipuram.
  • Pallavas spread Indian culture to South-East Asia (dominant in Cambodia till the 8th c.); the Pallava-style Shikhara appears in Java, Cambodia, Annam. Last ruler Aparajitavarman defeated by Chola Aditya I in 897 AD, ending the dynasty.
  • Pallava Art — began the Dravida style, later perfected by the Cholas — 4 stages: Mahendravarmana Group (600–630, Bhairavkonda cave, Undavalli's Ananteswar temple); Mammala Group (630–668, Mandapa/Ratha temples incl. Sapt Pagodas at Mamallapuram); Rajasimha Group (680–720, Kailashnatha & Vaikunth Perumal temples Kanchi, Shore Temple Mamallapuram); Aparajit Group (879–897, Mukteshwar/Matangeshwar Kanchi, Parshuramesvar Gudimallam).
  • Sculpture indebted to Buddhist tradition — monumental, linear, less ornate than Deccan work; best example "Descent of the Ganges"/"Arjuna's Penance," Mamallapuram.
  • Monuments: Shore Temple (UNESCO 1984, Shiva, built by Narasimhavarman II/Rajasimha, Mahabalipuram); Kailasanathar temple (granite, Shiva, Narasimhavarman II — began standing rock-cut architecture); Seven Ratha/Pancha Rathas (built by Narasimhavarman I; "Seven Pagodas" per Marco Polo) — Draupadi, Arjuna, Bhima, Dharmaraja, Nakula-Sahadeva Rathas.
TRAPVatapikonda is Narasimhavarman I's title for conquering Vatapi (642 AD) — do not confuse with Pulakeshin II, who was on the receiving end of that defeat, or with "Dakshinapatheshwara" (Pulakeshin II's own title for beating Harsha).

  • Amoghavarsha I (814–878 AD): son of Govinda III (real name Sarva); title "Nripatunga"; converted to Jainism; patronised Acharya Jinasena (Adi Purana) and grammarian Saktayana (Amoghavritti). Himself authored "Kavirajamarga" (earliest known Kannada work) & "Prashnottara Ratnamalika." Called "Ashoka of the South" for his non-violence/Jain leanings.
  • Krishna II: territorial stagnation, resisted by Eastern Chalukyas & Cholas. Indra III (914–928 AD): briefly restored supremacy, occupied Kannauj, defeated Pratihara Mahipala I; titles "Ratta Kandarpa," "Nityavarsha"; described by Arab traveller Al-Masudi in "Muruj ul-Zuhab."
  • ★ Krishna III (939–967 AD), last great Rashtrakuta ruler: captured Tanjore, defeating Chola Prantaka I at the Battle of Tokkolam (949 AD); title "Akhilakshtrakuta Chakravartin"; controlled Gujarat-to-Tanjore at his peak.
  • Khottiga (967–972 AD), also known as Karka II: defeated by Tailapa II (founder of the Western/Later Chalukyas of Kalyani); Manyakheta sacked by Paramara Siyaka II, starting the terminal decline. Indra IV (973–982 AD), last ruler, died by Sallekhana (Jain fasting-death, cf. Chandragupta Maurya in Ch.4 (Jainism & Buddhism)). Power passed to Tailapa II's Western Chalukyas.
  • Rashtrakuta Architecture: Ellora caves (34 total — 1–12 Buddhist, 13–29 Hindu, 30–34 Digambar Jain); Kailasa Temple (Krishna I, at the 16th Ellora cave, world's largest monolithic rock-cut temple, UNESCO 1983 with Ajanta-Ellora); Elephanta Caves (7 caves, Rashtrakuta+Kalachuri, Trimurti idol of Shiva, UNESCO 1987); Dashavatara Temple (Vishnu, bridges Gupta→post-Gupta temple styles).

1Tripartite Struggle: Pala Dynasty (Bengal)

CROSS-REFThe Rashtrakutas' own role in this struggle (Dhruva Dharavarsha, Govinda III, Indra III's campaigns vs. the Pratiharas/Palas) is already built in Ch.9 (Post-Gupta Dynasties) §4 — not repeated here. This batch introduces the Pala & Pratihara sides properly.
  • A prolonged 8th–10th c. AD fight for Kannauj (symbol of imperial legitimacy), also called the "Kannauj Triangle War," among the ★ Palas (East India/Bengal), Pratiharas (W & Central India — Rajasthan/MP/Gujarat) and Rashtrakutas (Deccan).
  • Gopala (750–770 AD), founder: elected by local leaders to end anarchy in Bengal; founded Odantapuri University; followers of Mahayana Buddhism.
  • Dharmapala (770–810 AD): most expansionist Pala ruler, title "Paramsaugat." Founded Vikramashila University (Bhagalpur, Bihar) & Sompura University. Defeated Indrayudh, conquered Kannauj briefly, installed puppet ruler Chakrayudh → title "Uttarapath Swami." Defeated by Dhruva Dharavarsha (Rashtrakuta) near Munger.
  • Devapala (810–850 AD): expanded the empire to its greatest extent (Assam, Odisha, parts of Deccan); sent Buddhist missions to Sri Lanka/Burma/Java. Shailendra king Balaputradeva got land grants for a Nalanda monastery from him. Notable monks: Dipankar Atisha, Dharmasvami.
  • ★ Mahipala I (988–1038 AD): revived the dynasty after decline, recovering N/E Bengal & Bihar; mentioned in Kalhana's Rajatarangini. Successors: Nayapala → Vigrahapala III → Mahipala II → Ramapala (last to try reviving the empire) faced attacks from the Kalachuri and Sena dynasties → Madana Pala, defeated by Vijayasena of the Sena Dynasty — end of Pala rule in Bengal.
  • Sena Dynasty (succeeded Palas): founder Hemanta Sena, real founder Vijayasena, capital Vijayapura. Under Sena rule Jaydeva wrote "Gita Govinda." Ballala Sena introduced "Kulinism"; built the Dhakeshwari Temple, Dhaka. In 1204 AD Bakhtiyar Khilji attacked Lakshman Sena, ending the Sena Dynasty.

2Gurjara-Pratihara Dynasty

  • Nagabhatta I (730–760 AD), founder: capital Kannauj/Bhinmal; defeated Arab forces near Broach, Rajasthan (8th c.).
  • Vatsaraja: 1st Pratihara king in the Tripartite Struggle; defeated by Dharmapala. Nagabhatta II: recovered Kannauj, defeated Dharmapala but lost to Govinda III (Rashtrakuta). Ramabhadra: weak, short reign.
  • ★ Mihir Bhoja/Bhoja I (836–885 AD), the greatest Pratihara ruler: extended the empire Gujarat→Bengal; title "Adivaraha" (Boar/Vishnu), Varaha-image coins; called "Enemy of Islam" by Arab travellers (e.g. Sulaiman). Gwalior Inscription records his win over Govinda III at Sagar Tal (Rashtrakutas' own records claim the opposite — a genuine cross-dynasty disagreement) and his defeat of Devapala (Pala), reasserting supremacy over Kannauj.
  • Mahendrapala (885–910 AD): son of Bhoja I; ruled Punjab-to-Bengal; lost a campaign against Kashmir's Shankaravarman; patronised poet ★ Rajashekhara, "Kalidasa of the Pratiharas" (works: Karpuramanjari, Viddhasalabhanjika, Bal Ramayana, Bal Mahabharata, Harivilasa, Bhuvanakosha, Kavyamimamsa).
  • Mahipala I (Pratihara — not the Pala king of the same name in §1): defeated by Indra III (Rashtrakuta) ~915 CE, losing Kannauj — start of Pratihara decline. Bhoja II: weak, internal conflicts. Vijayapala: lost many territories to rival powers during the decline. Rajyapala: offered no resistance to Mahmud of Ghazni's 1018 CE invasion, later killed by Chandela ruler Vidyadhara for capitulating. Trilochanapala: ruled during the state's collapse — external invasions + internal fragmentation ended the dynasty, and with it the Tripartite Struggle itself.

4Chola administration & temples

  • Administration: king + Council of Ministers; empire divided Mandalam→Valandu (district)→Nadu (sub-district)→Kurram (group of villages)→Ur (village). Known for decentralised local/village government.
  • ★ Uttaramerur inscription (mentions Parantaka I): villages divided into 30 wards (kudumbus), each run by a local committee (Variyam); two assemblies — Ur (commoners) & Sabha (learned Brahmins). Sabha's specialised committees: Eri Variyam (irrigation), Tottavariyam (gardens), Pon Variyam (gold/finance), Nyaya Variyam (justice). Members picked by the Kudavolai (lottery) system; only property/land owners could stand; 3-year terms.
  • Land donation types: Brahmadeya (Brahmins), Vellanvagai (peasants), Devadana (temples), Pallichchhandam (Jain community), Shalabhoga (schools). Taxes: Vetti (forced labour), Kadamai (land revenue).
  • ★ Brihadeeshwara/Rajarajeshwara Temple, Tanjore — built by Raja Raja I, UNESCO 1987; capstone/kumbam on its vimana is a monolithic ~81-ton granite piece. Gangaikonda Cholapuram temple — Rajendra I, UNESCO 2004. Airavatesvara Temple (Darasuram) — Rajaraja II, "temple in a chariot" design, UNESCO 2004. All 3 are "Great Living Chola Temples," built with the interlocking stone method (no mortar).
  • Nataraja (dancing Shiva, Tandava pose) — bronze, lost-wax/Cire perdue casting, standing on the right leg; the Cholas were famous for such bronzes. Dakshinmurti Shiva portrait found at Brihadeeshwara Temple; the figure under Shiva's feet = "Apasmara," demon of ignorance/ego. Rajendra I's temple also displays war-trophy statues seized from the Chalukyas, Eastern Chalukyas, Kalinga and the Palas — one from nearly every rival dynasty in this batch.

5Temple architecture (Nagara/Dravida/Vesara + regional styles)

  • ★ Three broad styles: Nagara (North India), ★ Dravida (South India — the Chola style), Vesara (Nagara+Dravida mix) — directly tested twice (Misc-03 Q19 & Q22).
  • Nagara: shrine = Shikhara (curvilinear/conical); no Gopuram; Nandi placed inside, chief deity at the Garbhagriha (sanctum sanctorum); built on a square jagati (Panchayatan style). 3 Shikhara sub-types: Rekha Prasad/Latina (walls curve inward, e.g. Lakshmana Temple, Khajuraho); Phamsana (stacked slabs rising to a point); Valabhi (rectangular, vaulted roof, e.g. Teli Ka Mandir, Gwalior).
  • Dravida: shrine = Vimana; Gopuram (entrance) present; Nandi placed outside, chief deity also at the Garbhagriha; apsidal/elliptical plan = Gaja Pristha; large temple-tank reservoirs.
  • Other dynasties' temples: Kandariya Mahadeva Temple (Khajuraho, M.P.) — Chandela Dynasty, Kamasutra-themed wall carvings. Dilwara Jain Temple (Mt. Abu, built by the Vastupala brothers) & Modhera Sun Temple & Somnath Temple (Gujarat) — Solanki Dynasty, Maru Gurjara style (Modhera by King Bhimadeva I).
  • Kalinga Architecture (Odisha) — 3 types: Rekha Deula (tall curvilinear tower), Pidha Deula (stepped pyramid mandapa), Khakhara Deula (rectangular, wagon-vaulted). Vaital Deula (Bhubaneswar) — Khakhara type. ★ Konark Sun Temple — "Black Pagoda," built by Narasingha Deva I (Eastern Ganga), Nagara+Kalinga/Pidha style, on the ₹10 note; 24 stone wheels = hours of the day, 7 horses = days of the week. Lingaraj Temple — Somavamsi Dynasty (Jajati/Yayati Keshari), Rekha+Pidha combo. Jagannath Puri Temple — "White Pagoda," Rekha style, built by Anantavarman Chodaganga Deva (Eastern Ganga). Mukteshvara Temple — Somavamsi, Rekha+Pidha, dedicated to Shiva.

6Sangam Age (3rd c. BC – 3rd c. AD)

  • South India skipped a Bronze Age, going straight Neolithic→Megalithic Age (2500–500 BC); structures: Dolmen (graves), Menhir (standing stone); pottery: black-and-red ware; pastoral community.
  • "Sangam" = Assemblies; covers the area south of the Krishna & Tungabhadra rivers. ★ 3 Sangams held, all patronised by the Pandyas, called Muchchangam: 1st — Madurai, chaired by Rishi Agastya, no surviving text; 2nd — Kapadapuram/Alvai, chaired by Tolkappiyar, only surviving work ★ Tolkappiyam (earliest Tamil grammar); 3rd — (North) Madurai, chaired by Nakkirar, most works survive (Ettutogai, Pattupattu, Patinenkilakanakku).
  • Sangam Literature: narrative form = Melkannakku (18 major works); didactic form (Love/Akam, War/Puram) = Kilkanakku (18 minor works, e.g. Tirukkural). Epics: Silappadikaram (Ilango Adigal — merchant Kovalan, his wife Kannagi, courtesan Madhavi); Manimekalai (Chithalai Chathanar/Sittalai Sattanar — sequel, about Kovalan & Madhavi's daughter, Buddhism-themed); Sivaga Sindamani (Jain-themed); Bharatam (Perudevanar).

A.2 Where sources disagree

  • Mihir Bhoja vs. Govinda III at Sagar Tal: Parmar's own Gwalior Inscription note says Bhoja's inscription records a victory over Govinda III's army, "though Rashtrakutas claim the opposite" in their own records — a genuine, acknowledged two-sided dynastic-inscription dispute, not resolved here (both dynasties' own sources naturally claim their own king won).
  • Sangam Age date range: Parmar gives 3rd century BC – 3rd century AD; Lucent gives a narrower 1st–3rd century AD for the same period. Not a hard conflict — Parmar's wider range simply extends back to cover the sparsely-attested 1st Sangam, which Lucent doesn't emphasise separately.
