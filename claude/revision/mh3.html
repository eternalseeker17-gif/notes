Colonial impact (Lucent): introduced market economy, ended customary rights; cash-revenue encouraged money-lending; sharpened social differentiation (rich had court access); forced commercial-crop cultivation; broke up village-society stability.

2Economic Impact of British Rule

Three phases (Lucent): Mercantile (1757–1813, EIC political power monopolised trade) → Industrial (1813–58, Act of 1813 one-way trade, India flooded with cheap British imports, forced raw-material export/finished-goods import) → Financial (1860 onward, India as market+raw-material supplier; Railways/Post/Telegraph 1853, Avadh Commercial Bank 1881, Tata Iron & Steel 1907).

Drain of Wealth — Dadabhai Naoroji (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901); R.C. Dutt (Economic History of India, Vol I 1901/Vol II 1903). Constituents: company-servant extortion; "investment" (Bengal-revenue-funded exports); duty-free British trade; official remittances/salaries; "home charges" (pensions paid from Indian treasury); interest to British investors. Effects: stunted Indian enterprise, financed British capitalism, kept India as an uncompetitive free-trade zone, foreign-managed interlocking capitalist firms.

HOOK★Railways introduced 1853 not for philanthropy but colonial interest: raw materials to ports, British goods into Indian markets, troop movement (esp. after 1857).

5The Revolt of 1857

5.1 Causes

  • Political: ★Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie, 1848) — Satara(1848), Sambalpur/Jaitpur(1849), Udaipur(1852), Jhansi(1853), Nagpur(1854); Awadh annexed 1856 (misgovernance) — "Nursery of the British Army," last Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, son Birjis Qadr proclaimed new Nawab; Nana Sahib's pension refused (adopted son of Peshwa Baji Rao II).
  • Economic: heavy taxation, forced evictions, discriminatory tariffs, destruction of traditional handicrafts.
  • Socio-religious: Sati abolition (1829), widow remarriage (1856) — hurt orthodox sentiment.
  • Military: discrimination against Indian soldiers; General Service Enlistment Act (1856, Canning) mandated overseas service (loss of caste).
  • Immediate cause: ★Enfield rifle cartridges greased with cow/pig fat.
HOOKMangal Pandey timeline: 29 Mar attacked officers, Barrackpore → 8 Apr hanged → 9 May 85 Meerut sepoys jailed → 10 May Meerut revolt begins → 11 May Delhi seized, Bahadur Shah II proclaimed Emperor.

Full suppression: Gwalior, 20 June 1858. Nature-of-revolt views: "Sepoy Mutiny" (Syed Ahmed Khan, R.C. Majumdar, John Seeley) vs. "War of Independence" (V.D. Savarkar, Karl Marx, K.M. Panikkar). Failure causes: disunity/poor coordination; princely states (Scindia, Holkar, Nizam) aided the British; limited to North/Central/Western India.

5.3 Aftermath — Government of India Act 1858

  • East India Company abolished; Crown Rule via Queen's Proclamation (Queen Victoria); Secretary of State for India created.
  • Governor-General also titled Viceroy — 1st Viceroy: Lord Canning.
  • Doctrine of Lapse withdrawn; princely states assured against annexation; army restructured (fewer Indian soldiers) on the Peel Commission's recommendation; "divide and rule" pursued thereafter.
HOOKHistoriography quotes: Savarkar — "the First War of Independence." R.C. Majumdar — neither "First," nor "National," nor "a War of Independence."

6PYQ-book-only additions (not in either source book)

  • ★ Khongjom War (1891) — Anglo-Manipuri War against British annexation of Manipur; led by Yuvraj Tikendrajit Singh, who commanded the Manipuri army; publicly hanged 13 Aug 1891 on charges of murder/rebellion.
  • ★ Peer Ali Khan — a Patna bookseller who led rebellion in Patna during 1857, attacked Gulzarbagh (3 July 1857), killed opium agent Dr. Lyell; publicly hanged without trial, 7 July 1857, by Commissioner William Tayler.
  • ★ Bhima Nayak, "Robin Hood of Nimar" — led tribal rebellion & guerrilla resistance in the Satpura region of Madhya Pradesh during 1857, per the PYQ book. DOUBT The raw extraction's own "Bhil Revolt" entry instead places a "Bhima Naik" in Khandesh, Maharashtra (1818) — possibly the same person under two different regional traditions/spellings, or two distinct Bhil leaders; not reconciled here.
  • After the Indigo Revolt (1859–60), planters shifted their operations to Bihar as Bengal indigo production declined — directly connects to Champaran (1917).
  • British forced Assam peasants to cultivate tea (also indigo in Bengal, wheat in Punjab) to meet European demand.
