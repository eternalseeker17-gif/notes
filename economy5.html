1Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — Parmar Ch.6

Recommended by the Royal Commission on Indian Currency & Finance, chaired by Hilton Young, 1926. Established under the RBI Act, 1934; began operations 1 April 1935, Calcutta; nationalised 1 Jan 1949; HQ moved to Mumbai, 1937. First Governor: Sir Osborne Smith; first Indian Governor: Sir C.D. Deshmukh. RBI's emblem features a tiger and a palm tree.

HOOK RBI Governors' chronology ★: Dr. Y.V. Reddy (2003-08) → Dr. D. Subbarao (2008-13) → [Raghuram Rajan/Urjit Patel gap] → Dr. Urjit R. Patel (2016-18) → [Shaktikanta Das] → Shri Sanjay Malhotra (2024/25-present, per Lucent's "as on 29.05.2025" table) ★ — a tested chronological-ordering question.

Central Board of Directors — apex governing body under RBI Act 1934, appointed by GOI for 4 years. Comprises: the Governor (Chairperson), 4 full-time Deputy Governors, 10 non-official directors from various fields, 2 Government of India officials (Ministry of Finance), 4 directors representing the Local Boards. Banking Ombudsman scheme introduced by RBI in 1995; Fixed Rate Repo announced by Govt in 1997.

Lead Bank Scheme ★ — launched by RBI, December 1969 ★; assigns one specific public/private bank to lead banking-and-credit development in a district, promoting an "Area Approach" to rural credit, financial inclusion and priority sector lending.

Functions of RBI: (1) Regulator of Banks under the Banking Regulation Act 1949 — "Bank of Banks"; grants banking licences, regulates CRR/SLR, influences interest rates via repo rate. (2) Monetary Authority — formulates/implements monetary policy to control inflation, manage money supply, ensure credit flow. (3) Currency Printing — prints/manages banknotes (except ₹1 note and coins, issued by Ministry of Finance). (4) "Lender of Last Resort" — final funding source for commercial banks in a crisis. Currency is printed at Nasik, Dewas, Mysore, Salboni; coins minted at Mumbai, Hyderabad, Calcutta, Noida.

The Bimal Jalan Committee (2018-19), appointed by RBI, recommended a new Economic Capital Framework (ECF) enabling transfer of surplus reserves to govt while maintaining a Contingency Risk Buffer (CRB) of 5.5%–6.5% of RBI's balance sheet.

Clean Note Policy — introduced by RBI in 1999, under Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act 1949; banks must not pass soiled/torn/scribbled notes to customers and must deposit them with RBI instead.

Per RBI Act 1934, banking is classified as: Scheduled Banks (Commercial → for-profit → Public Sector [12 Nationalised Banks, as printed] / Private Sector [Indian e.g. HDFC/Kotak; Foreign e.g. Citigroup/HSBC]) and Non-Scheduled Banks (Cooperative → non-profit, "depositors are owners"). SBI was the only nationalised bank until 14 banks (deposits >₹50 crore) were nationalised via The Banking Companies (Acquisition & Transfer of Undertaking) Act, 1969; later 6 banks (Andhra Bank, Corporation Bank, New Bank of India, Oriental Bank of Commerce, Punjab & Sind Bank, Vijaya Bank) nationalised via the 1980 Act (deposits >₹200 crore).

HOOK Bank nationalisation, 19 July 1969 ★ — 14 major private banks nationalised to extend rural banking, ensure agri/small-industry credit, reduce private monopoly ★.

History of Banking System in India

Bank of Hindustan — first bank, established 1770, Calcutta. Three Presidency Banks during British rule — Bank of Calcutta (1806), Bank of Bombay (1840), Bank of Madras (1843) — merged into a single Imperial Bank of India in 1921, later nationalised and renamed State Bank of India, 1955. First Indian-owned bank: Allahabad Bank, 1865. Regional Rural Banks (RRB) established as a statutory body under RRB Act 1976 for rural financial activities (e.g. Grameen Banks); Pratham Grameen Bank set up 2 October 1975, Moradabad, UP (India's first RRB).

RRB ownership: Central Govt 50% (largest stakeholder), sponsor commercial bank 35%, State Govt 15%; supervised by NABARD, regulated by RBI; set up on Narasimham Committee on Rural Credit recommendations. RRB consolidation began 2005 following the Dr. Vyas Committee (2001). The Kelkar Committee (1986) recommended strengthening RRBs — raising authorised capital to ₹5 crore, paid-up capital to ₹1 crore, directing surplus into profitable investments, and merging weak RRBs.

Functions of Commercial Banks — Primary: accepting deposits (savings/current/fixed) and providing loans/advances (cash credit, overdraft, bill discounting). Secondary: agency services (cheque collection, bill payment, acting as agent) and general utility services (locker facility, demand drafts, forex, cards, online banking).

Microfinance Institutions (MFI) — provide micro-credit/loan/savings/insurance/remittance services to low-income populations; collateral-free microfinance loans to households with income up to ₹3 lakh/annum. Father of Microfinance: Muhammad Yunus (2006 Nobel Peace Prize, creator of Grameen Bank). First MFI in India: Self Employed Women Association (SEWA) Bank, Ahmedabad, 1974.

Self Help Groups (SHG): 10–20 BPL people forming a group; provide collateral-free loans at lower interest than the informal sector; empower women/marginalised groups. Joint Liability Groups (JLG): 4–10 people forming a group to jointly apply for a bank loan; offered to individuals, tenant farmers, sharecroppers, small/marginal farmers — collateral-free.

Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana (PMMY) — promotes financial inclusion via micro-credit/loans to small businesses: Shishu (up to ₹50,000), Kishor (₹50,000–5 lakh), Tarun (₹5–10 lakh), Tarun Plus (₹10–20 lakh). MUDRA (Micro Units Development & Refinance Agency Ltd.) launched 8 April 2015, receives 100% funding from SIDBI.

NBFCs (Non-Banking Financial Companies/Institutions) — offer banking-like services without a banking licence; registered under the Companies Act 1956, regulated by RBI under RBI Act 1934; cannot accept demand deposits or issue cheques; provide Gold Loans; deposits NOT government-guaranteed; not required to maintain CRR/SLR. NBFC-MFI — registered and regulated by RBI, provide micro loans/financial services to low-income rural individuals; established on Malegam Committee (2010) recommendations. RBI cap: non-NBFC-MFI entities' microfinance-loan limit is 25% of total assets; to qualify as NBFC-MFI, 75% of total assets must be microfinance loans (revised 2022, was earlier 85%).

Capital requirements for NBFC-MFI: minimum Net Owned Fund (NOF) — General (all-India) ₹5 crore; North-East Region ₹2 crore (lower, to encourage financial inclusion). Payment & Settlement Systems Act, 2007 — all payment systems (UPI, NEFT, RTGS, Credit Cards) regulated by RBI under this Act.

Priority Sector Lending (PSL): Commercial Banks (domestic & foreign) must allocate 40% of ANBC (Adjusted Net Bank Credit) to PSL; RRBs & Small Finance Banks — 75% of ANBC; NBFCs generally have no specific PSL targets. Sub-targets: Agriculture 18%, Weaker Sections (SC/ST/Women) 12%. Shortfalls go to the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF), managed by NABARD. Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) operate under both RBI and the Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RCS).

5Monetary Policy — Parmar Ch.7

Monetary policy = RBI's regulation of money supply and interest rates to maintain price stability, control inflation, ensure growth, using quantitative and qualitative instruments.

Balance Sheet: Liabilities = Deposits (Demand Deposits: savings/current accounts; Time/Term Deposits: FDs/RDs); Assets = Loans granted by the bank. Net Demand and Time Liability (NDTL) = total deposits determining lendable funds and reserve requirements: Demand Liabilities (withdrawable anytime — savings/current) and Time Liabilities (fixed tenure — FDs/RDs).

Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) ★
Kept in cash with the RBI; no interest earnedKept in liquid assets — cash, gold, or approved securities ★; interest earned
TRAP ↑CRR/SLR reduces banks' lending capacity ★ (they must set aside more, less left to lend) — NOT increase it (a tested Assertion-Reason flips this).

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) — established on Urjit Patel Committee recommendations; 6 members: 3 from RBI (Governor as Chairman, Deputy Governor, an Executive) + 3 from Govt of India; meets bimonthly to decide repo rate/CRR changes.

Monetary Policy Tools

Quantitative Tools — influence overall money supply/credit:

  1. CRR (see above)
  2. SLR (see above)
  3. Bank Rate (BR) — rate at which RBI lends long-term to commercial banks without collateral; higher BR → costlier borrowing → ↓money supply; lower BR → cheaper loans → ↑liquidity
  4. Open Market Operations (OMO) — also called sterilization: (i) Outright Purchase — RBI directly buys govt securities, no repurchase agreement; (ii) Repurchase Obligation ("Rere") — banks borrow from RBI using govt securities as collateral with a promise to repurchase = Repo; RBI inviting banks to deposit surplus funds against govt securities = Reverse Repo. Repo + SDF (Special Depository Facility) together = Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF). SDF lets banks deposit excess funds with RBI without any collateral.
  5. Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) — emergency overnight borrowing by banks from RBI at a higher rate than repo; penalty for failing to maintain CRR/SLR = 3% above Bank Rate per day of default; uses G-Secs within the SLR quota; only scheduled commercial banks can access it. Base Rate = minimum rate below which banks cannot lend.

If banks can't maintain CRR/SLR, they borrow from other banks via the Interbank Lending Market at the Call Money Rate (decided on the basis of RRR and MSF) — short-term overnight interbank loans; limit: 2% of NDTL. MCLR (Marginal Cost of Funds Based Lending Rate) — minimum lending rate, introduced by RBI in April 2016, for transparent/policy-responsive loan pricing. Money Multiplier (MM) = 1/CRR — highest possible increase in money supply from a change in bank deposits; NBFCs/NBFC-MFIs need not maintain CRR/SLR; ↑CRR → banks hold more reserves, lend less → fewer loans → less money creation → lower multiplier (and vice versa).

SituationRBI ActionEffectPolicy Stance
Inflation↑ CRR, SLR, BR, RRR, RR↓ money supplyContractionary/Tight/Dear/Hawkish

HOOK Expansionary Monetary Policy ★ = central bank cuts rates to stimulate growth/spending during a slowdown (the exact tested term is "expansionary", not contractionary/QE labels). Debt Monetisation ★ = RBI directly buys govt bonds/securities to fund the fiscal deficit, printing new currency → raises money supply → often high inflation ★.

Qualitative Monetary Policy Tools — selective control over credit direction: (1) Changing Margin Requirements — ↑margin for non-essential sectors, ↓ for priority sectors like agriculture; (2) Consumer Credit Regulation — RBI sets loan-instalment/down-payment/repayment rules in advance; (3) Moral Suasion — persuasion/implicit threats by RBI to influence bank behaviour; (4) Credit Rationing — RBI fixes credit supply per bank and per sector to prevent excessive lending.

Fiscal Policy — government's tax/spending/borrowing strategy to control inflation/deflation, unemployment, growth. To control inflation: ↑Taxes (lowers disposable income → lowers demand), ↓Subsidies (raises prices → discourages excess consumption), ↓MSP (Minimum Support Price — discourages excess crop production, keeps food prices in check). Monetary Policy = RBI's tool; Fiscal Policy = Government's tool.

6Banking Part 2 — Liquidity, Money Functions & Aggregates (Parmar Ch.8)

Liquidity = ease of converting an asset to cash without losing value; money has the highest liquidity. Liquidity order: Cash > Gold > Cheque > Bond; Demand Deposit more liquid than Term Deposit. Money was invented to solve the barter system's problems: Double Coincidence of Wants, Lack of Divisibility, Lack of Store of Value.

Functions of Money: Primary — Medium of Exchange (buying/selling). Secondary — Medium of Value/Unit of Account: (a) Standard of Deferred Payment, (b) Store of Value (money retains value over time, unlike perishables; has no intrinsic value unlike gold; paper money has value from govt backing), (c) Highly Portable and Durable.

HOOK Reasons to hold money ★: Transaction Motive (daily spending — groceries, transport, bills), Precautionary Motive (emergency expenses — illness, accidents), Speculative Motive (by J.M. Keynes) — investment timing, e.g. buying stocks on a dip.

Liquidity Preference Theory ★ = Keynes's theory of demand for money — interest rates are determined by demand/supply of money, not just savings/investment. Cambridge Cash Balance Approach ★ — developed by Marshall, Pigou, and Keynes; value of money tied to the demand to hold cash balances (store of value) rather than transaction velocity.

Paradox of Thrift (Keynesian) — individuals increasing savings in a downturn collectively reduces aggregate demand and total economy-wide savings, as lower consumption reduces business revenue and income.

HOOK Speculative Demand for Money ∝ 1/Interest Rate — inverse relationship: rates ↑ → people invest in bonds/financial assets, hold less cash → speculative demand ↓; rates ↓ → returns low, hold more cash → speculative demand ↑. "Interest rates are an opportunity cost/'price' of holding money balance in an economy." Liquidity Trap ★ = interest rates so low that the liquidity-preference curve's horizontal portion appears — speculative money-demand becomes infinitely/perfectly elastic — monetary policy becomes ineffective.

Types of Money in the Economy & Money Aggregates

  1. Currency in Circulation — Currency with Banks (daily operations) + Currency with Public (spending money). CDR (Currency Deposit Ratio) = Money held by public in currency / Money held by public in deposits.
  2. Deposits with Banks — Demand Deposits (savings/current, withdrawable anytime) + Term Deposits (FDs/RDs, fixed period).
  3. Deposits with RBI — Bank Deposits with RBI (CRR, mandatory % of NDTL) + Other Deposits with RBI (govt institutions, foreign central banks, international financial orgs, some private entities).
  4. Postal Deposits — similar to bank Term/Demand Deposits, managed by the Post Office Savings Bank (POSB).

Money Market, Capital Market & Assets Classification

Money Market (short-term loans, <1 year): Call Money — repaid within 1 day; Notice Money — repaid in 2–14 days. Capital Market (long-term loans, >1 year): stocks/bonds for long-term investment. Treasury Bills (issued by GOI through RBI) — also called Zero Coupon Securities; issued in 3 tenures (91/182/364 days); classified as money-market instruments (maturity <1 year); always issued at a discount, no interest paid.

Assets classification: Regular Assets — generate consistent returns, interest+principal repaid on time. Stressed Assets — non/potentially-non-performing, overdue <90 days. Non-Performing Assets (NPAs) — overdue >90 days: Substandard (>90 days but <12 months), Doubtful (>12 months), Loss Assets (uncollectible). Wilful Defaulter — has capacity to repay but deliberately doesn't. Bad Bank — a specialised entity handling NPAs, buying them and recovering value; 2021 Budget announced two bad banks: National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL) and India Debt Resolution Company Limited (IDRCL).

SARFAESI Act, 2002 (Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Securities Interest Act) — lets banks seize defaulters' properties. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 — resolves the Twin Balance Sheet Problem (stressed banks + stressed corporates) via the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP); moratorium ("calm period") of 180 days (extendable to 270 days). Governing bodies: NCLT (initial insolvency cases) and NCLAT (appeals against NCLT decisions).

7Lucent §9 — Indian Financial System

The Indian Financial System channels funds between demand side (individual investors, businesses, government) and supply side (banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, other financial institutions). It has two parts: the Indian money market (short-term funds) and the Indian capital market (medium/long-term funds); performs a crucial role in economic development via saving-investment (capital formation).

Classified into: (1) Industrial finance, (2) Agricultural finance, (3) Development finance, (4) Government finance. Devaluation = lowering the local currency's official value vs. foreign currency/gold. Balance of Payments (BoP) = systematic record of all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world, divided into current account and capital account. Balance of Trade (BoT) = value of exports − value of imports (goods only, services excluded). EXIM Policy 2000-01 introduced the SEZ Scheme. In 1994-95 the Indian Rupee was made fully convertible on the current account. Fiscal Policy = policy on public revenue, expenditure and allied matters.

Indian money market: organised sector (RBI at apex + Public/Private/Foreign Sector Banks + other financial institutions) and unorganised sector (indigenous bankers + NBFCs).

RBI — established under the RBI Act 1934, 1 April 1935, with capital of ₹5 crore; nationalised 1 January 1949 on the recommendation of a Parliamentary Committee (1948); is the supreme monetary/banking authority, keeps commercial banks' reserves; its financial year runs 1 July–30 June (as printed).

Indian Capital Market — market for long-term capital; includes (1) Government Securities (Gilt-edged market), (2) Industrial Securities Market, (3) Development financial institutions (IFCI, IDBI, ICICI, SFCs, IIBI, UTI), (4) Financial Intermediaries (Merchant banks). Trends driven by institutional-investor activity and corporate-sector results.

8Lucent §11 — Money, Banking and Insurance in India

RBI established 1 April 1935, nationalised 1 January 1949. Finance Ministry issues ₹1 notes and all coins; RBI issues all other currency notes. First Indian-managed limited-liability bank: Oudh Commercial Bank (1881); Punjab National Bank established 1894. The 1906 Swadeshi movement spurred formation of many commercial banks. The Banking Companies Act (Feb 1949) was later amended to become the Banking Regulation Act, 1949.

Reforms in Banking Sector

Jan-Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile (JAM) linkage, interoperable Bank Mitras, UPI and DBTs expanded digital transactions. PSB reform agenda = EASE (Enhanced Access & Service Excellence) Reforms — governance, prudential lending, risk management, technology, HR outcomes. Amalgamation improved sector efficacy via economies of scale, cheaper fund-raising, stronger financial capacity, tech adoption. Amendments to the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 protect bona-fide banking decisions from inquiry without approval, supporting prudent risk-taking. Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act 2020 strengthened co-operative bank regulation (professionalism, capital access, governance, sound banking via RBI). IBC used for large stressed-account resolution; ARC regulatory framework amended for transparency/governance. (Source: BHARAT 2025)

Digital Banking Units (DBUs) — announced in Union Budget 2022-23; 75 DBUs in 75 districts dedicated by the PM on 16 October 2022 to mark 75 years of independence (Azadi Ka Amrit Mahotsav).

UPI — indigenous digital payment system, multi-bank single-app transfers. Grew from 92 crore transactions (FY2017-18) to 13,116 crore (FY2023-24), CAGR 129%. Live internationally in UAE, Bhutan, Nepal, Singapore, Mauritius, France, Sri Lanka; RuPay card acceptance live in Nepal, Bhutan, Singapore, Mauritius, UAE. (Source: BHARAT 2025)

Scheduled/Non-Scheduled Banks, RRBs, Co-operative & Development Banks

Scheduled banks — listed in RBI Act 1934's second schedule; paid-up capital + reserves ≥₹5 lakh, RBI-satisfied depositor interest. All commercial (Indian/foreign), RRBs and state co-operative banks are scheduled. Non-scheduled banks — not in the second schedule; only one such bank currently exists in India.

RRBs — first established 2 October 1975; RRB Act 1976 to complement cooperative credit for rural/agri credit; ownership: GOI 50%, State Govt 15%, sponsor bank 35%; targets small/marginal farmers, agri labourers, rural artisans, small entrepreneurs. Total RRBs: 43 (DoFS). Per Economic Survey 2024-25: RRBs have 22,069 branches across 700 districts, 26 states, 3 UTs.

Co-operative Banks — organised under state Co-operative Credit Societies laws; mainly benefit agriculture/rural sector; first established 1904; two kinds — agricultural (dominant) and non-agricultural. Structure: State Co-operative Bank (apex) → Central Co-operative Banks (district) → Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (village); long-term agri credit via Land Development Banks. 1991: Narasimham Committee constituted to advise on banking-system reconstruction.

Development Banks: IDBI (1964) — finance to large/medium industry. IFCI (1948) — project finance + promotional services. ICICI (1955) — term loans in Indian/foreign currency, underwriting shares/debentures. SIDBI (1989, as printed here — vs "1990" elsewhere in the source) — assistance to small-scale industries via state finance/industrial corporations, commercial banks. EXIM Bank (1982) — wholly GOI-owned, finances/facilitates India's foreign trade; authorised capital raised to ₹20,000 crore; Net Worth (as on 31.12.2023) ₹22,299 crore.

Housing, Rural & International Financial Institutions

National Housing Bank (NHB) — started 1988; develops housing finance. NABARD — established 1982, initial capital ₹100 crore; paid-up capital ₹17,080 crore against authorised ₹30,000 crore (as on 31 March 2024); apex refinancing agency for agricultural-finance institutions.

New Development Bank (NDB) — instituted 2015 to fund infrastructure/sustainable development in emerging economies; founding members Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa (BRICS); USD 1 billion initial capital contribution.

Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) — set up 2016, Multilateral Development Bank for Asian infrastructure/sustainable development; India a founding member and 2nd-largest shareholder; India + 20 other countries signed the founding MoU in Beijing.

IFAD (International Fund for Agricultural Development) — set up 1977, 13th specialised UN agency; fights rural poverty/hunger; 176 member countries in 3 lists (A: Developed, B: Oil-Producing, C: Developing); India is in List-C, a founder member, contributed US$147.0 million.

IMF — India a founder member; 188 member nations; Board of Governors has 1 Governor + 1 Alternate Governor per country; India's Finance Minister is ex-officio Governor, RBI Governor is Alternate Governor; India's IMF constituency also includes Bangladesh, Bhutan, Sri Lanka. (Source: INDIA 2024)

World Bank — India a founder member of IBRD (189 members) which with IDA (173 members) forms the "World Bank"; IBRD serves middle-income/creditworthy low-income countries; IDA serves the poorest countries via "credits"/grants. India borrows for poverty reduction, infrastructure, disaster mitigation, rural development. (Source: INDIA 2024)

Asian Development Bank (ADB) — established 1966, India a founding member; 68 members (49 regional + 19 non-regional); HQ Manila; India held 6.32% shares (6,72,030 shares) with 5.35% voting rights (as on 31 Dec 2021); India began borrowing from ADB in 1986. (Source: ADB)

Insurance Sector

Ltd, General Insurance Corporation of India Ltd, Agriculture Insurance Company of India Ltd, Export Credit Guarantee Corporation of India Ltd. Industry growth: ~11% CAGR over 5 years; total premium ~₹10 lakh crore (FY2022-23); AUM ~₹60 lakh crore (March 2023). (Source: BHARAT 2025)

Insurance = spreading the loss of a few over many; two sectors — Life & General Insurance. Life insurance introduced by the British; Oriental Life Insurance Company, Calcutta, 1818 (British firm). LIC established September 1956; GIC established November 1972. Indian insurance has low rural penetration, low turnover/profitability despite high premiums. Committee on Insurance Sector Reforms set up 1993 under R.N. Malhotra, report 1994. Sector opened to private participation via the IRDA Act, 1999; IRDA HQ Hyderabad.

Post Office Financial Services & Postal/Rural Postal Life Insurance

Department of Posts runs Small Savings Schemes for the Ministry of Finance; POSB serves via 1.57 lakh post offices; operates Savings Accounts, RD, TD, MIS, PPF, NSC, KVP, SCSS, Sukanya Samriddhi Account. Important Banking Terminology (Lucent glossary, full 16-term list)

  1. Bank Rate — rate at which the central bank finances commercial banks; a short-term tool; upward revision signals banks to raise deposit/base rates too.
  2. Basis Points — 1 percentage point = 100 basis points (e.g. 2%→3% = 100 bps rise).
  3. LRR (Legal Reserve Ratio) = CRR + SLR. CRR — funds banks must keep with RBI; ↑CRR drains excess money from banks.
  4. SLR — reserve a bank must hold as cash/gold/approved govt securities before lending; controls credit expansion, ensures solvency, controls inflation/propels growth via money-supply control.
  5. Repo Rate — rate at which commercial banks borrow rupees from RBI; ↓repo → cheaper bank borrowing; ↑repo → costlier.
  6. Reverse Repo Rate — rate at which RBI borrows from commercial banks; "always 1% less than the Repo Rate" (as printed — contradicted by the Dec-2023 rate table above, see DOUBT).
  7. NEFT (National Electronic Fund Transfer) — slower than RTGS, RBI-mediated, any amount transferable (even ₹1).
  8. RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement) — real-time, gross-basis fund/security transfer, no waiting period; minimum ₹2 lakh, no upper limit.
  9. LAF (Liquidity Adjustment Facility) — RBI tool for day-to-day liquidity mismatches via repo/reverse-repo operations.
  10. MSF — overnight bank borrowing from RBI against G-Secs; "always 1% more than the Repo Rate" (as printed — see DOUBT above).
  11. NOSTRO Account — an Indian bank's account maintained abroad.
  12. VOSTRO Account — a foreign bank's account maintained in India with its correspondent bank.
  13. CRAR — capital / aggregated risk-weighted assets (credit + market + operational risk).
  14. SDR (Special Drawing Rights) — international reserve asset created by the IMF in 1967; value based on a currency portfolio; accounting entries, not physical/hard currency or gold.
  15. BOND — publicly traded long-term debt security; issuer pays fixed interest over a period and repays principal at maturity.
  16. NPA (Non-Performing Asset) — a loan/leased asset that stops generating income for the bank; becomes NPA after 90 days of missed interest/principal payments.

NEFT details — began operations 21 November 2005, one settlement/day, 8 participating banks initially; grew to 216 banks with half-hourly batches (8am–7pm); handles ~10.5 million transactions/day averaging ~₹80,000 crore. Processing charges waived from 1 July 2019. 24×7 NEFT from 16 December 2019 (barring a half-hour post-midnight break in some cases).

Currency, Coinage & Demonetisation

SPMCIL (Security Printing and Minting Corporation of India Ltd.) — the only PSU under the Department of Economic Affairs; formed by corporatising 9 units (4 mints, 4 presses — 2 currency-note + 2 security presses, 1 paper mill); incorporated 2006 under the Companies Act 1956; manufactures security paper, mints coins, prints currency/bank notes, non-judicial stamp papers, postage stamps, travel documents. Supplies currency/coins to RBI, stamp papers to states, postal stationery to the Postal Dept, passports/visa stickers to MEA, plus commemorative coins and MICR/non-MICR cheques. In 2010, SPMCIL formed a 50:50 JV with BRBNMPL named Bank Note Paper Mill India Pvt Ltd (BNPMIPL) — a greenfield bank-note paper mill, capacity 12,000 MT/year (two 6,000 MT lines). (Source: BHARAT 2025)

Demonetisation — GOI demonetised ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes on 8 November 2016; these Mahatma Gandhi-series notes ceased legal tender from 9 November 2016; new ₹500 and ₹2,000 Mahatma Gandhi New Series notes issued in exchange; ₹100/50/20/10/5 notes remained legal tender, unaffected. Stated aims: curbing counterfeiting/terror-funding and cracking down on black money. (Source: INDIA 2021)
