1Poverty — Concept, Types & Estimation Lineage (Parmar Ch.9)

Poverty = lacking financial resources/essentials for a basic standard of living; diverse causes/effects (environmental, legal, social, economic, political). Types: Absolute (lacking food/water/shelter/clothing/healthcare/education), Relative (income far below society's average), Subjective (feeling poor regardless of income, driven by comparison/expectation/psychology). Poverty Gap Index measures the depth of poverty (how far below the line). World Poverty Clock — managed by World Data Lab, real-time global poverty estimates. Poverty estimation in India = NITI Aayog's task force, using NSSO data (under MoSPI); BPL = income/spending below a set minimum.

National MPI (NITI Aayog, Nov 2021) uses 12 indicators (adds antenatal care + bank account) — India's multidimensional poverty declining since 2005-06; 24.82 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty in 9 years; UP = most people lifted out; Bihar = highest MPI (most poor), Kerala = lowest MPI.

2Poverty in India — Lucent §3 (Full History & Latest World Bank Data)

Indian Economy's characteristics (Lucent §3): (1) Agrarian Economy — 54.6% of population in agriculture/allied activities (Census 2011), contributing 17.8% of GVA in 2019-20 (INDIA 2023). (2) Mixed Economy — blend of public/private, moving toward a capitalist/market economy post-liberalisation. (3) Developing Economy — high structural unemployment (a person is "employed" if working 273 days/year, 8 hrs/day); low savings due to low income + high consumption; 2nd most populous nation (2001-2011 growth 17.69%, ~1.85 crore added/year; 2011 population 121.07 crore = 17.5% of world population on just 2.42% of world land); lacks large-scale modern industrialisation.

Primary sector = agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining/quarrying; Secondary = industry, manufacturing, electricity/gas/water/utilities, construction; Tertiary = business, transport, communication, all services. Best indicator of economic development = per capita income. Key development factors: natural resources, capital gain, skilled labour, agri surplus sale, justified social organisation, political freedom, freedom from corruption, technological knowledge/education. WPI covers 3 major groups: primary articles, fuel & power, manufactured products.

Measuring Poverty in India (history): First official rural/urban poverty lines introduced 1979 by the Y.K. Alagh Committee. 1993 — D.T. Lakdawala Committee extended lines to states. 2005 — govt appointed the Tendulkar Committee (rural line seen as too low); reported 2009, revised the rural line upward. 2012 — Rangarajan Committee appointed after media criticism; reported June 2014, recommended raising both rural/urban lines further — decision still pending, so the Tendulkar line remains the official poverty line (basis for 1993-94/2004-05/2011-12 estimates). (Source: NITI Aayog)

Six official poverty committees since Independence: Planning Commission Working Group (1962), V.M. Dandekar & N. Rath (1971), the 1979 Task Force/"Separate Committee" (Alagh), Lakdawala Committee (1993), Tendulkar Committee (2009), Rangarajan Committee (2014).

BPL statistics (2011-12): Rangarajan Committee — 30.95% rural, 26.4% urban BPL, vs Tendulkar methodology's 25.7% rural / 13.7% urban (Source: The Indian Express, 4 July 2015 & Economic Survey 2015-16). Separately, ~22% of Indians live BPL — 25.7% rural, 13.7% urban (per RBI, Source: Financial Express, 21 Sept 2019).

HOOK World Bank's Spring 2025 Poverty and Equity Brief — India's extreme poverty (int'l benchmark $2.15/day) fell sharply from 16.2% (2011-12) to 2.3% (2022-23), lifting 171 million people out of extreme poverty. Rural extreme poverty: 18.4%→2.8%; Urban: 10.7%→1.1%. At the $3.65/day (lower-middle-income) line: 61.8%→28.1% (378 million lifted); Rural 69%→32.5%, Urban 43.5%→17.2%; rural-urban gap narrowed 25→15 points (7% annual decline). Non-monetary/MPI poverty: 53.8% (2005-06)→16.4% (2019-21)→15.5% (World Bank's measure, 2022-23). With 2021 PPPs and revised international lines, 2022-23 rates are projected at 5.3% (extreme) and 23.9% (lower-middle-income). India's consumption-based Gini index improved from 28.8 (2011-12) to 25.5 (2022-23).

PEBs (Poverty and Equity Briefs) — World Bank publication, twice yearly (Spring + Annual Meetings), covering 100+ developing countries; uses national poverty lines plus international benchmarks $2.15 (extreme), $3.65 (lower-middle-income), $6.85 (upper-middle-income).

Employment trends (World Bank, since 2021-22): employment growth has outpaced working-age population growth, especially for women; Urban unemployment fell to 6.6% in Q1 FY24/25 (lowest since 2017-18); first male rural-to-urban worker shift since 2018-19; rural female agri-employment growth; rising self-employment, especially rural women. (Source: PIB, MoIB, 26 April 2025)

4The 1991 BoP Crisis

TRAP 1991 crisis = shortage of foreign exchange ★ (not water/electricity/labour). By June 1991, reserves fell to ~$1-1.1 billion ★ — enough for only ~2 weeks of imports ★ (petroleum/essentials). Causes: fiscal deficit, foreign-debt repayment failure, Gulf War oil-price spike, inflation ★ — NOT natural disaster or migration (tested distractors).

BoP/currency crisis occurs when a nation can't pay for essential imports or repay foreign debt. India faced this in 1991 under PM P.V. Narasimha Rao, FM Manmohan Singh; the government responded with broad reforms under the LPG model (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation).

India approached the IMF and World Bank ★ for a $7 billion loan ★ (via IBRD) — conditions: structural reforms under SAP (Structural Adjustment Programme) — privatisation, trade liberalisation, fiscal discipline, FDI opening ★.

HOOK Rupee devalued in two stages, July 1991 ★, cumulative ~18-20% against USD ★ — objective: cheaper/competitive exports, discourage imports, boost forex inflow ★. Devaluation → improved export competitiveness ★ (weaker rupee = cheaper Indian goods abroad — a tested Assertion-Reason, both true, R explains A).

Rupee made fully convertible on Current Account, 1994 ★ (RBI, under IMF Article VIII) — Current Account Convertibility = freedom to convert rupee for trade transactions (imports/exports/remittances/travel). Capital Account Convertibility = freedom to convert for capital transactions (foreign investment, loans, overseas asset purchase) — in India, only partially convertible, regulated by RBI.

Tarapore Committee — constituted 1997 by RBI (reviewed again 2006) under S.S. Tarapore; examined Capital Account Convertibility (CAC) feasibility; recommended a phased roadmap subject to preconditions — low fiscal deficit, controlled inflation, strong banking sector (low NPAs), stable financial system.

5FOREX Reserves, IMF/World Bank & Related Institutions

Forex reserves = assets held by a nation's central bank/monetary authority for import cover and shock protection. 4 components: Foreign Currency Assets (FCA), Gold reserves, Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) (a.k.a. "Paper Gold"), Reserve Tranche Position in the IMF (higher IMF quota = higher voting power). SDR basket currencies: US Dollar, Euro, Yen, Yuan, Pound.

HOOK Bretton Woods Conference, 1944 (officially the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference) — 44 nations, 730 delegates → created IMF + World Bank (Twin Organisations), HQ Washington D.C.; also created the IBRD (now part of World Bank). Bretton Woods system collapsed 1971 (US suspended gold convertibility). India ranks 8th in IMF voting power. Top IMF borrowers: Argentina, Egypt, Ukraine, Pakistan, Ecuador.

The Washington Consensus — 10 free-market policy prescriptions (John Williamson, 1989) for crisis-prone developing nations (esp. Latin America), promoted by IMF/World Bank/US Treasury: privatisation, deregulation, trade liberalisation, fiscal discipline. IMF major reports: World Economic Outlook, Global Financial Stability Report, Fiscal Monitor, External Sector Report.

Lorenz Curve — graphs income/wealth distribution within a population, measuring inequality; Gini Coefficient 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality).

Exchange rate regime evolution in India: 1928-1948 — Rupee pegged to British Pound Sterling; Post-1948 (IMF system) — pegged to US Dollar (Fixed Exchange Rate); 1975 — delinked from Pound Sterling; 1992 — LERMS (Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, dual rate: part govt-controlled, part market); 1993 — fully Market-Determined/Floating Exchange Rate System.

NEER (Nominal Effective Exchange Rate) — weighted average of domestic currency vs. a trade-weighted basket, no inflation adjustment. REER (Real Effective Exchange Rate) — NEER adjusted for inflation, shows real external competitiveness.

Types of Currency: Hard (stable, widely accepted — USD, Euro), Soft (weak, not widely accepted), Hot (moves quickly for short-term profit on rate changes), Heated (under heavy demand/speculation), Cheap (depreciated/low value), Dear (appreciated/high value).

7Tax Evasion, Money Laundering & Anti-Avoidance (Parmar Ch.9)

BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting): Base Erosion = shell companies across jurisdictions break the audit trail, reducing taxable profit in a high-tax jurisdiction; Profit Shifting = moving those "eroded" profits to a low/no-tax jurisdiction (e.g. Cayman Islands, Bahamas) with little real economic activity. Round Tripping = domestic black money sent abroad then brought back as FDI via shell companies/tax havens to appear legitimate.

FATF (Financial Action Task Force) — HQ Paris, founded July 1989 (G7); sets global anti-money-laundering/terror-financing standards. Three Stages of Money Laundering: (1) Placement — illegal funds enter the financial system; (2) Layering — concealing origin, separating from source; (3) Integration — funds fully absorbed into the economy.

Shell Company — a paper-only corporate entity with little/no real business, used to hold assets, route funds, or hide real ownership. Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT) — Section 115JB, Income Tax Act 1961; ensures companies with high exemptions still pay minimum tax when normal tax liability is below 15% of "book profit" (per financial statements, calculated per Companies Act 2013); applies to all domestic/foreign companies; prevents "zero-tax" profitable companies.

9Lucent §15 — Trade and Commerce: History & Foreign Trade Evolution

Indian trade was highly developed in ancient times. After the British East India Company (1600), trade favoured India till 1757 (Company bought cloth/spices for costly metals). The British then imposed heavy duty on cloth to destroy India's industry; post-Industrial-Revolution Britain flooded world markets with cheap goods, driving colonisation. British monopoly on cotton sale made Indian weavers' raw material costlier — by 1813 the Indian handloom business was ruined. Late 19th century: power-machine-based modern (textile) industries emerged in India.

Pre-independence foreign trade ran on colonial principles; post-independence, India adopted inward-looking policies based on import replacement. India's world-trade share: 1.78% (1950) → 0.6% (1995, persisting through 1997-98 per Economic Survey 2001-02). By FY2019-20, India was the 18th-largest exporter (1.7% share, INDIA 2021) and 10th-largest importer (2.6% share, INDIA 2020) of merchandise.

Foreign Trade Policy 2023 & SEZs

FTP 2023 — launched April 2023; blueprint for integrating India with global markets; shifts from incentive-based regime to an enabling ecosystem ("Aatmanirbhar Bharat", "Local goes Global"); highlights: incentive→tax-remission shift, tech-driven trade facilitation, e-commerce exports, districts as export hubs; targets USD 2 trillion in goods+services exports by 2030.

SEZs — Asia's first EPZ set up at Kandla, 1965; 7 more EPZs followed; SEZ Act 2005 (with SEZ Rules) effective 10 February 2006. All 8 pre-existing EPZs (Kandla & Surat-Gujarat, Santa Cruz-Maharashtra, Cochin-Kerala, Chennai-Tamil Nadu, Visakhapatnam-Andhra Pradesh, Falta-West Bengal, Noida-UP) converted to SEZs; SEZs under the 2005 Act are primarily private-investment driven. 275 operational SEZs as of June 2023 (166 IT/ITES + 109 multisector).

Composition of Foreign Trade, External Sector & Trade Performance (FY25 data)

Imports classified as Bulk (POL + non-POL — consumption goods, fertilizers, iron/steel) and Non-bulk (capital goods incl. electrical/non-electrical machinery, pearls/precious stones, other items). Structural import shifts since 1951: rising capital-goods/raw-material imports (industrialisation), rising raw-material imports (import liberalisation for export promotion), declining food-grain/consumer-goods imports (self-sufficiency). Exports classified into 4 categories: (1) Agriculture & allied (coffee, tea, oil cakes, tobacco, cashew, spices, raw cotton, rice, fish, meat, vegetable oils, fruits/vegetables/pulses); (2) Ores & minerals (manganese ore, mica, iron ore); (3) Manufactured goods (textiles/garments, jute, leather/footwear, handicrafts, chemicals, engineering goods, iron/steel); (4) Mineral fuels & lubricants. Over the years, agri's export share has declined while manufactured goods' share has risen.

External Sector (ES 2024-25) — total exports (Apr-Dec FY25) reached USD 602.6 bn (+6%; +10.4% excl. petroleum/gems-jewellery); total imports USD 682.2 bn (+6.9%). Forex reserves USD 640.3 bn (end-Dec 2024), covering ~90% of external debt (USD 711.8 bn, Sept 2024).

Global trade (UNCTAD, Dec 2024) — merchandise export/import indices grew 3.5%/3% YoY (Q3 2024); services exports/imports grew 7.9%/6.7% YoY. Global trade projected to hit ~USD 33 trillion in 2024 (services +7% YoY, goods +~2%), expanding by ~USD 1 trillion (3.3%) overall.

India's trade trend (Apr-Dec FY25) — exports USD 602.6 bn (+6% YoY); imports USD 682.2 bn (+6.9% YoY); overall trade deficit rose from USD 69.7 bn to USD 79.5 bn YoY. Non-petroleum exports +7.1%; non-petroleum-non-gems-jewellery exports +9.1%. Sectoral export growth: drugs/pharma +6.4%, electronic goods +28.6%, engineering goods +9.9%, chemicals +5%, textiles +7.6%. Merchandise exports grew a modest 1.6% YoY (petroleum-product value decline offsetting gains). Merchandise imports +5.2% (non-oil-non-gold imports: USD 352.1 bn vs USD 340.5 bn); merchandise trade deficit widened to USD 210.8 bn (from USD 189.7 bn).

Textile Exports — India = 6th-largest global textile/apparel exporter; sector contributes 2.3% of GDP, 13% of industrial production, 12% of exports; employs 45+ million directly. 2023 textile exports: USD 34 bn (apparel 42%/USD 14.6 bn, raw/semi-finished 34%/USD 11.7 bn, finished non-apparel 30%/USD 7.8 bn); Europe+US took ~66% of apparel exports, 58% of finished non-apparel, 12% of raw materials. Globally, MMF (man-made fibre) = 77% of fibre consumption (2024) vs cotton's 22%; India's MMF production share = 9.2% globally, with room to catch up to Vietnam/China/Taiwan.

Export diversification (1994-2022) — India is now a top exporter of shipping vessels (~33% market share) and a leading exporter of iron/steel alloys (an unexplored market pre-1994).

E-commerce exports — global B2C e-commerce: USD 5.7 trillion (2022)→USD 8.1 trillion (2026E), 9.1% CAGR; India's B2C market: USD 83 bn (2022)→USD 150 bn (2026E), 15.9% CAGR (still only ~1.5-2% of the global market); e-commerce exports ~USD 4-5 bn (FY23), projected USD 200-300 bn by 2030.

Ease-of-doing-business for exporters — logistics-hub development under the National Logistics Policy; paperless e-BRC system saves >₹125 crore/year on 2.5 million e-BRCs; DGFT's 'Trade Connect e-Platform' — single-window market-access tool, especially for MSMEs.

BoP, FDI & Forex (Economic Survey 2024-25 figures)

India's BoP stayed stable (resilient services exports, benign crude prices, FPI/FDI revival). CAD moderated to 1.2% of GDP (Q2 FY25) vs 1.3% (Q2 FY24); merchandise trade deficit rose to USD 75.3 bn (Q2 FY25) from USD 64.5 bn (Q2 FY24).

FDI — gross inflows rose from USD 47.2 bn (first 8 months FY24) to USD 55.6 bn (FY25, +17.9% YoY). Cumulative FDI (Apr 2000–Sept 2024) crossed USD 1 trillion (USD 1,033.4 bn per DPIIT). Sector-wise (H1 FY25 equity inflows): Services 19.1%, computer software/hardware 14.1%, trading 9.1%, non-conventional energy 7%, cement/gypsum 6.1%. Net FDI (first 8 months, RBI): FY25 = USD 0.48 bn vs FY24 = USD 8.5 bn; full-year FY24 net FDI = USD 10.1 bn; FY23 = USD 19.8 bn.

Forex reserves — comprise FCA, gold, SDRs, RTP in IMF. After crossing USD 700 bn, reserves moderated to USD 640.3 bn (end-Dec 2024), covering ~90% of external debt (USD 711.8 bn, Sept 2024). India ranks 4th globally in forex reserves (after China, Japan, Switzerland), with a 2024 increase of USD 27.1 bn. Reserves stood at $688 bn as of April 2025.

Exchange Rate, Inflation & Outlook (Economic Survey 2024-25)

INR is market-determined (no target/band); factors: Dollar Index, capital flows, interest rates, crude prices, CAD. In the first 9 months of FY25 (to 6 Jan 2025), INR depreciated a modest 2.9% — better than CAD (5.4%), KRW (8.2%), BRL (17.4%). The Euro is the official currency of 20/27 EU states (the "Eurozone").

Global inflation (IMF WEO, Jan 2025) — peaked 8.7% (2022) → 5.7% (2024). India's retail inflation moderated 5.4% (FY24) → 4.9% (FY25, Apr-Dec); food = ~2/5 of India's CPI basket, so CFPI drives much of headline inflation. Core inflation fell 0.9 points (FY24→FY25); WPI was in deflation (-0.7%, FY24), staying low in FY25. RBI's MPC (Dec 2024) revised its FY25 inflation projection from 4.5% to 4.8%; expects 4.2% in FY26 (normal monsoon assumed). IMF projects 4.4% (FY25) / 4.1% (FY26) inflation for India. World Bank (Oct 2024) expects commodity prices to fall 5.1% (2025) / 1.7% (2026).

Medium-term outlook — IMF WEO (Oct 2024 update) projects India to become a USD 5 trillion economy by FY28, reaching USD 6.307 trillion by FY30; MoSPI's first advance estimate puts FY25 growth at 6.4% (constant prices).

Geo-Economic Fragmentation — since the 1980s: global trade rose from 39% of world GDP (1980) to 60% (2012); global FDI inflows grew from USD 54 bn (1980) to >USD 1.5 trillion (2019); world economy grew from USD 11 trillion (1980) to >USD 100 trillion (2022, nominal); extreme poverty ($2.15/day) fell from 42% (1981) to 8.4% (2019), driven by China/India growth; global population grew 4.4 bn (1980)→8 bn (2022), urbanisation 39%→57%; internet penetration rose from ~0 (1980) to 5.3 bn people/66% (2022).

Export Credit & Rupee/Gold Data

NIRVIK Scheme — introduced by ECGC (Export Credit Guarantee Corporation of India); full name: Export Credit Insurance Scheme (ECIS); covers up to 90% of principal+interest, both pre- and post-shipment credit; aims to enhance credit accessibility/affordability for exporters.

Rupee exchange rate (29 May 2025): 1 USD = ₹85.44; 1 GBP = ₹115.07; 1 Euro = ₹96.27.

RBI's Gold Reserve — added ~58 tonnes of gold in FY25 (data released 5 May 2025); total holding = 879.59 tonnes (26 tonnes added in H2 FY25); gold ≈ 12% of total forex reserves (up from 9.32% in Sept 2024 to 11.70% by end-March, in dollar-value terms). Of the H2 addition, 348.62 tonnes held with the Bank of England/BIS, 18.98 tonnes as gold deposits; 58.20% of gold held in local vaults (March-end) vs 59.72% (Sept 2024) — domestic storage share rose from 38% to 59% since March 2023 (per RBI report). India's forex reserves stood at $688 bn (April 2025). (Source: Business Standard, 5 May 2025)

Trade Organisations — Full History

IMF — established 27 December 1945 (per the Bretton Woods decision); began functioning 1 March 1947; 188 member countries (2017); promotes financial/economic cooperation and extends world trade. IBRD — established 1945; with the International Finance Corporation, International Development Agency, and Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency, together called the "World Bank"; helps capital investment and long-term balanced development. GATT — came into being 30 October 1947, functioning from 1 January 1948; principle: equal-tariffs policy, removing quantitative bans, democratic dispute resolution. WTO — took GATT's place 1 January 1995; HQ Geneva; 164 members (April 2018); India a founder member.

India-ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement — signed 13 August 2009, effective 1 January 2010; created one of the world's largest Free Trade Areas — ~1.8 billion people, combined GDP ~USD 2.75 trillion.
