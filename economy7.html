7Lucent §6 — Planning: Types, Models & the Plan Table

Economic Planning = skillful use of limited natural resources to achieve desired goals; India's concept derives from the USSR; 'Planning' derives objectives/social premises from the Constitution's Directive Principles of State Policy. 1934 — Visvesvaraya's "Planned Economy for India" first raised planning; 1938 — All India Congress Committee demanded it; 1944 — 8 industrialists produced the Bombay Plan, plus the Gandhian Plan (Sriman Narayan) and the People's Plan (M.N. Roy, labour leader, April 1944); 30 Jan 1950 — Sarvodaya Plan (Jai Prakash Narayan). Post-Independence: 1947 — a committee on economic planning formed under Nehru; on its recommendation, the Planning Commission was constituted March 1950 (non-constitutional, advisory; the Constitution did not provide for it); the first Five-Year Plan's format was prepared in 1951. NITI Aayog replaced it 1 January 2015.

Annual Plans (1966-69) — three Annual Plans formulated within the draft 4th-Plan framework. Eighth Plan (per Lucent) — could not launch in 1990 due to political instability; new govt (June 1991) set it to commence 1 April 1992, treating 1990-91 and 1991-92 as separate Annual Plans focused on maximising employment and social transformation.

Types of Planning

  • Imperative Planning — a Central Planning authority decides every aspect of the economy; targets/processes strictly followed; used in socialist economies.
  • Indicative Planning — the State sets broad parameters/goals without micromanaging; adopted in India from the 8th Five-Year Plan, as practised in many developed countries.
  • Perspective Planning — long-period (15-20 years) planning; operationalised through Five-Year and Annual Plans; sets the broad direction for the economy.
  • Rolling Plan — three simultaneous layers: (1) current-year plan incl. the annual budget, (2) a fixed-multi-year plan (e.g. 3/4/5 years, revised annually), (3) a 10/15/20-year perspective plan.
  • Core Plan — Planning Commission asks states for projected revenue estimates, then fixes State Annual Plan expenditure heads accordingly — keeps plan targets realistic, prevents fund diversion from priority items.

Models of Economic Development

Nehru-Mahalanobis Model — drove development strategy from the 2nd Five-Year Plan through the 1980s; aimed to enlarge opportunities for the underprivileged; "growth with social justice" — a self-generating development path removing poverty/unemployment/disease/ignorance; the State controlled the economy's commanding heights via the public sector.

Gandhian Model of Growth — the 'Gandhian Plan' was brought out by Acharya S.N. Agarwala in 1944, reaffirmed 1948; aims to raise the material and cultural level of the Indian masses to a basic standard of life; emphasises scientific agricultural development and rapid growth of cottage/village industries — agriculture as the most important planning sector (vs. Nehru's heavy-industry primacy), supported by handicrafts and cottage industries.

LPG Model of Development — introduced 1991 by FM Dr. Manmohan Singh; charted a new strategy emphasising Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation; bigger private-sector role; envisages much larger FDI; aims at export-led growth (vs. earlier import substitution); reduces the State's role, abandoning "planning fundamentalism" for a liberal, market-driven pattern.

PURA Model of Development — Union Cabinet approved PURA (Providing Urban Amenities in Rural Areas) in principle on 20 January 2004, to bridge the rural-urban divide; championed by Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam to eradicate poverty (Mahatma Gandhi had earlier flagged rural exploitation by urban society); aims at economic development without population transfer, creating social/economic infrastructure for private investment in rural areas; neo-Gandhian — brings modern technology/amenities to rural areas while emphasising employment enlargement, reconciling employment and GDP-growth goals. The 11th Plan (2007-12) allocated ₹248 crore for PURA in a Public-Private Partnership mode.

8Lucent §8 — New Economic Policy: Full Detail

New Economic Policy = reforms in production pattern, new technology adoption, fuller capacity use. First devised/implemented in 1985 under Rajiv Gandhi; a "second wave" came in 1991 under P.V. Narasimha Rao, driven by the Gulf War and the BoP problem. 3 main objectives: Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation. Main sectors of the 1991 reform: Fiscal Policy, Monetary Policy, "Value Fixation Policy" (as printed), Foreign Policy, Industrial Policy, Foreign Investment Policy, Business Policy, Public Sector Policy.

First Generation Reforms — macro-level stabilisation: liberalisation/deregulation of industry, financial-sector reforms, taxation reforms. Second Generation Reforms — micro-level structural change: labour reforms, land reforms, capital-market reforms, expenditure and power-sector reforms. Since the reforms, poverty fell from 36% (1993) to 26% (end of 10th Plan) — but inequality rose (World Bank Report 1999-2000 confirms this). By 2005-06 the policy had become progressively more open/liberal/global.

Disinvestment = reducing government's industry share. The Disinvestment Commission was constituted 1996 to review/advise/regulate disinvestment; first Chairman: G.V. Ramakrishna (printed "Ramkrishna" in one instance). In 1992, the National Renewal Fund was set up to rehabilitate labourers displaced by industrial modernisation of sick units. "Navratna" — a globally-rising company, given complete autonomy by government to encourage it. Second-phase reform aim: eradicate poverty and achieve 7-8% growth.

9Lucent §13 — Industry: Full IPR 1991 Detail, CPSEs & MSMEs

India began its post-independence industrial quest in 1947; IPR 1948 marked the start of Indian industrial policy (accepted both public/private sector importance, handed basic-industry development to the Public Sector); IPR 1956 gave the public sector a strategic/pre-eminent role while allowing private-sector co-existence; objective — develop public + cooperative sectors, control private monopoly. IPR 1948's 4 industry categories were reduced to 3 in IPR 1956. 1973 — Joint Sector constituted on Dutta Committee recommendations. IPR 1980 (per Lucent) was influenced by federalism, giving concessions to agriculture-based industries. Comprehensive liberalisation steps were declared in the Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 — privatisation and liberalisation as its main thrust.

Major Features of NIP (New Industrial Policy), 1991 — full detail

  • Abolition of industrial licensing — for all industries irrespective of investment size, except those involving security/strategic concerns, social reasons, safety/environmental issues, hazardous products, or elitist-consumption articles.
  • Easier foreign investment/technology entry — govt encouraged foreign trading companies to help Indian exporters; direct foreign investment approved up to 51% foreign equity in high-priority industries.
  • Diluted public-sector role — of the 17 industries reserved for the public sector since 1956, all but 4 areas were removed from the Reserved List: (1) arms/ammunition and defence equipment/aircraft/warships, (2) atomic energy, (3) minerals specified under the Atomic Energy (Control of Production and Use) Order 1953, (4) railways.
  • MRTP Act changes — firms with assets above ₹100 crore (since 1985) were classified as MRTP firms needing case-by-case approval to enter selected industries; NIP 1991 scrapped this asset threshold. In 2002, the Competition Act replaced the MRTP Act, and the Competition Commission replaced the MRTP Commission.
  • Liberalised industrial-location policy — no central industrial approval needed outside cities of >1 million population (except compulsorily-licensed industries); in cities >1 million, non-polluting industries excepted, others must locate 25 km beyond the periphery.
  • Abolition of Phased Manufacturing Programmes for new projects (previously forced indigenisation in engineering/electronics industries).
  • Mandatory convertibility clause removed — earlier, banks/FIs financing industrial investment could convert part of their loans into equity (seen as an unwarranted takeover threat to private firms); NIP 1991 abolished this mandatory clause.

Navratna origin — Union Budget 1997-98 named 9 well-performing PSUs "Navratna" with full autonomy: SAIL, IOC, BPCL, HPCL, BEL, HAL, ONGC, NTPC (8 named in the source's own list, as printed). Later additions: GAIL (Aug 1984 — as printed, though this predates the 1997-98 announcement), MTNL, NMDC, PFC, PGCIL, REC, NALCO, SCI, CIL. Navratna PSUs get enhanced autonomy: uncapped capital-expenditure powers, technology joint ventures, domestic/international capital raising, financial joint ventures, wholly-owned subsidiaries.

Public Sector — Objectives & Disinvestment/Privatisation Distinction

Public Sector Enterprise (PSE) = government/public-owned undertakings; private sector = privately-owned. Main objectives of the Public Sector: promote rapid economic development via infrastructure creation/expansion; generate development finance; promote income/wealth redistribution; create employment; encourage small-scale/ancillary industries; promote exports and import substitution; promote balanced regional development.

Privatisation vs Disinvestment (Lucent's framing) — Privatisation = a change in ownership resulting in a change in management. Disinvestment = a wider term, from mere stake dilution up to a full ownership transfer (when govt stake falls below 51%). The Disinvestment Commission (Aug 1996, chairman G.V. Ramakrishna) advised: classify PSEs into core (max 49% disinvestment) and non-core (up to 74% disinvestment); distribute shares to small investors/employees for wide/worker ownership; give PSEs greater autonomy. Disinvestment policy has since evolved to include strategic disinvestment — selling 50%+ of shareholding with management-control transfer. To ease the PSU financial burden, govt introduced a Voluntary Retirement Scheme (VRS), nicknamed the "Golden Hand Shake Scheme", with full compensation to exiting employees.

Small Scale Industries & MSMEs

SSI importance in a labour-surplus economy: (1) Employment generation — labour-intensive vs capital-intensive large industry; (2) Equitable distribution — spread ownership across individuals/regions; (3) Mobilisation of small savings — runs on small capital; (4) MSME share in national GVA ≈ 32% (Economic Survey 2017-18); (5) Environment-friendly — dispersed away from urban centres. SSI problems: lack of timely/adequate/easy finance, raw-material shortages, weak marketing systems, competition from large-scale industry.

MSMEs contribute ~30% of India's GDP, ~46% of exports, employ 11.10+ crore people. New composite investment+turnover definition effective 1 July 2020 (Atmanirbhar Bharat package), identical limits for manufacturing and services:

Sick Industries — a unit/company (existing ≥5 years) whose accumulated losses equal/exceed its entire net worth by a financial year-end, or equal/exceed 50% of its average net worth over the preceding 4 years, and which has failed to repay creditor debts across 3 consecutive quarters on written demand.

10Lucent §14 — Industrial Performance, Infrastructure & Flagship Programmes

Index of Industrial Production (IIP) measures manufacturing/mining/electricity output, plus use-based categories (primary, capital, intermediate, infra/construction, consumer durables/non-durables). 2023-24 IIP growth: 5.9% overall (mining +7.5%, manufacturing +5.5%, electricity +7.1%).

Index of Eight Core Industries (ICI) — tracks coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilizers, steel, cement, electricity; combined weight ≈40.27% of IIP. Growth: avg (-0.6%) (2018-19 to 2020-21) → +10.4% (2021-22) → +7.8% (2022-23) → +7.6% (2023-24 provisional, steel/coal in double digits).

Sector-wise Performance

Coal — ~55% of India's energy needs; raw coal output: 716.08 MT (2020-21, provisional) vs 730.87 MT (2019-20); NITI Aayog's Draft National Energy Policy expects coal demand of 1.3-1.5 billion tonnes by 2030; plans for 30,000 Ha green cover (75 million trees) around coal areas by 2030; PSU renewable capacity 1,496 MW (as on 31.03.2021), with 5,560 MW more planned over 5 years.

Textiles — one of the world's largest industries, full value-chain manufacturing strength; T&A (incl. handicrafts) = 8.21% of India's total exports (2023-24). Contributes ~7% of industrial production, ~2% of GDP, ~15% of export earnings; employs ~45 million. India = largest producer of cotton and jute, 2nd-largest of silk and man-made fibre. Mill sector: 3,400 mills, 50+ million spindles, 8,42,000 rotors — world's 2nd largest. Kanpur (10 cotton mills) = "Manchester of North India".

Silk — ~97% of raw mulberry silk from Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal + J&K; called the "Queen of Textiles" (lustre/drape/strength); India is the only country producing all 4 commercial silk varieties — Mulberry, Eri, Tasar, Muga; 2nd-largest global producer (38,913 MT) after China.

Cotton — ~23% of global fibre production; ~60% of Indian textile industry's raw-material basket; sustains ~6 million cotton farmers + 40-50 million in related trades; largest cotton-cultivation area globally (~127 lakh hectares, ~38% of world area); 2023-24 output: 327.45 lakh bales.

Jute — largest global producer, ~80 lakh bales/year; provides direct employment to 0.37 million organised-mill workers.

Wool — India = 9th-largest wool producer (33.98 million kg raw wool, 2023-24): ~85% carpet-grade, 5% apparel-grade, 10% coarser-grade; average yield 0.9 kg/sheep vs world average 2.4 kg/sheep.

Steel — crude/finished steel production (Apr-Oct 2021-22): 66.91 MT / 62.37 MT (+25.0%/+28.9% YoY); finished-steel consumption 57.39 MT (+25.0%). Sponge-iron capacity 43.4 MT (2015-16); world's largest sponge-iron producer every year since 2003 except 2016; a net pig-iron exporter. Crude-steel capacity now 179.51 MT (BHARAT 2025).

Sectoral Developments — Electronics, Transport, Telecom, Power, Mining

Electronics — domestic production grew from ₹1.90 lakh crore (FY15) to ₹9.52 lakh crore (FY24), 17.5% CAGR; smartphone import-dependence cut to ~1% (99% domestic manufacture); ~33 crore mobile units produced in FY24, 75%+ 5G-enabled.

Road transport — total road network 63.4 lakh km (incl. National Highways 1,46,195 km — just 2% of the network carrying ~40% of road freight traffic); road transport = 78% of transport-services GVA (FY23), the highest share within transport.

Railways — 17 new Vande Bharat train-pairs + 228 coaches (Apr-Oct 2024). Gati Shakti Multi-modal Cargo Terminals: 91 commissioned, 234 locations approved (by 31 Oct 2024). Net-zero target: 30 GW renewable energy by 2029-30 (375 MW solar + 103 MW wind commissioned by Oct 2024). Economic corridors: 434 projects worth ₹11.17 lakh crore mapped on PM GatiShakti portal. Mumbai-Ahmedabad High-Speed Rail — sanctioned Dec 2015, 508 km, Japan-backed, revised cost ₹1.08 lakh crore, 47.17% physical progress (Oct 2024). Dedicated Freight Corridors — 2,741 km (96.4%) of the planned 2,843 km commissioned (Nov 2024). Kavach (indigenous Automated Train Protection) — ₹1,547 crore invested (till Nov 2024); Electronic Interlocking installed at 227 stations in FY25 (total 3,576 stations); first Direct Drive Interlocking commissioned Nov 2024 at Tajpur.

Civil Aviation — capex plan >₹91,000 crore (FY20-25), ~91% achieved by Nov 2024. UDAN (Regional Connectivity Scheme) — 619 routes, 88 airports (incl. 2 water aerodromes, 13 heliports) operationalised; airport cargo capacity reached 8.0 million MT in FY24.

Ports & Shipping — Sagarmala programme harnesses India's coastline/waterways for logistics efficiency (port connectivity, coastal community development, coastal shipping, inland water transport). Key projects: Vadhavan Mega Port (₹76,000+ crore, 9 container terminals); Tuticorin International Container Terminal (inaugurated Sept 2024, 6 lakh TEU/year capacity, vessels up to 10,000 TEU) + a planned Outer Harbour (+4 million TEU capacity, two 1,000m terminals); Chabahar Port (Shahid Beheshti Port, Iran) links Mumbai to Eurasia via INSTC — 43% rise in vessel traffic, 34% rise in container traffic (FY24); Sittwe Port, Myanmar (Kaladan Project) — alternative Kolkata-Mizoram route. River-cruise tourism: 82,587 day-cruise passengers (by Oct 2024); night-cruise passengers up 5x to 11,431 (FY24 vs FY19).

Telecommunications — 5G live in all states/UTs (by 31 Oct 2024), 779/783 districts, 4.6 lakh+ 5G BTS installed. BharatNet (broadband to all Gram Panchayats): 6.92 lakh km OFC laid, 2.14 lakh GPs service-ready (incl. 5,032 via satellite), 12.04 lakh FTTH connections (as of Dec 2024).

Power Sector — installed capacity +7.2% YoY to 456.7 GW (Nov 2024); renewable capacity +15.8% YoY to 209.4 GW (Dec 2024, from 180.8 GW Dec 2023) — renewables now ~47% of total installed capacity.

Manufacturing — industrial sector grew 6% in H1 FY25 (Q1: 8.3%, Q2 moderated "due to three key factors" — unspecified in the source). Mining — contributes ~8% of industrial output; top 5 states (Assam, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Odisha) = ~60% of all-State mining GSVA.

Kandla — First Green SEZ — Kandla SEZ (KASEZ) awarded IGBC Platinum Rating (26 July 2021) for green master planning/policy/infrastructure — first Green SEZ to achieve IGBC's Green Cities Platinum Rating for Existing Cities.

National Logistics Policy (NLP) — launched 2022; an interdisciplinary, cross-sectoral, multi-jurisdictional logistics framework, complementing PM GatiShakti (which targets integrated infrastructure, while NLP targets logistics-service efficiency via process streamlining, regulation, skill development, and technology adoption); vision: a tech-enabled, integrated, cost-efficient, resilient, sustainable, trusted logistics ecosystem.

Make in India — launched September 2014; facilitates investment, innovation, world-class infrastructure, positions India as a manufacturing/design/innovation hub ("Vocal for Local", "Make in India for the World"); now spans 27 sectors — DPIIT coordinates 15 manufacturing-sector plans, Dept. of Commerce coordinates 12 service-sector plans; investment outreach via ministries/states/Indian missions abroad.

Startup India — launched 2016; fosters entrepreneurship/innovation via 3 pillars — simplification & handholding, funding support & incentives, industry-academia partnership & incubation. By July 2024: 1,43,549 recognised startups across 56 industries, 670+ districts, at least one from every State/UT, 15.80+ lakh jobs created since 2016. Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS) — DPIIT scheme, ₹945 crore outlay, funds proof-of-concept/prototyping/trials/market-entry/commercialisation; targets ~3,600 entrepreneurs via 300 incubators over 4 years.
