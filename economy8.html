1Green Revolution — Reach & Regional Imbalance

HOOK Green Revolution (1960s) — HYV seeds, fertilizers, irrigation → self-sufficiency in foodgrains. Only effective where irrigation was already good ★ — Punjab, Haryana, western UP ★ (canal/tube-well irrigation, wheat-centric farming); largely ineffective in Bihar in its first phase ★ (flood-prone, weak infra, small landholdings, limited irrigation). Punjab = first & largest beneficiary ★.
TRAP Regional disparity cause ★ = HYV/irrigation access restricted to select states ★ — NOT price controls or fertilizer neglect (tested distractors). A tested Assertion-Reason confirms BOTH true, R correctly explains A ★ (regional imbalance ← HYV/irrigation initially available only in select states).

Green Revolution did NOT cover barley, ragi, minor millets — confined to rice, wheat, maize, jowar; biggest effect on wheat (~500% increase, per raw extraction). Credited internationally to Norman Borlaug ("Father of Green Revolution"); in India, Dr. M.S. Swaminathan played the leading role.

DOUBT Raw extraction's own internal inconsistency: Lucent p.257 says Green Revolution began under a "termed name" credited to "William Gande" (likely a garbled "William Gaud", the US administrator who coined the term "Green Revolution" in 1968) alongside Borlaug/Swaminathan; p.258 separately states it "started in the Third Five Year Plan" — this conflicts with Parmar Ch.10 (Batch 7), which places it in the Plan Holiday (1966-69). Both dates transcribed as printed, not resolved.

2Occupational Structure & Land Reforms (1947-1990)

HOOK At Independence (1947), ~70-75% of India's population depended on agriculture ★ (highest GDP contributor then, since manufacturing/services were nascent). By 1990, this had only fallen to ~65% ★ — despite agriculture's GDP share falling sharply, its workforce share barely moved: a tested "failure of structural transformation" ★ (workforce didn't shift to industry/services as theory predicts). A tested Assertion-Reason (planning 1951-90 failed to transform employment structure / >65% workforce still in agriculture) — both true, R explains A ★.

Occupational diversification ★ = the term for workers shifting from agriculture to industry/services (reduces rural risk, generates new income — NOT "industrialization"/"urbanization" alone, tested distractors).

HOOK Land reforms (1947-90) objective ★: abolish intermediaries (zamindars) + land ceilings ★ → redistribute surplus land to landless/small cultivators, reduce land-ownership concentration ★ — "land to the tiller" principle (NOT consolidate land in a few hands, promote cash crops, or mechanisation — tested distractors).

TRAP Fertilizer subsidies (1960-90) ★ were a "double-edged" instrument — boosted productivity but disproportionately benefited richer/larger farmers ★ (small farmers lacked resources to fully exploit them) — worsening rural inequality, NOT reducing employment or discouraging HYV use.

UP = highest share of workforce in agriculture ★ among the states tested (fertile Ganga-Yamuna plain, large rural population) — Punjab/Haryana >85% of net sown area under irrigation ★ (Bhakra Nangal canal system + tubewells).

7Agriculture — Growth, Production & National Data (Lucent §4, Full Coverage)

Indian agriculture grew at an average annual rate of 4.6% over the last 6 years (per source); 3.0% in 2021-22 vs 3.3% in 2020-21. India has rapidly become a net agri-exporter — agri/allied exports grew 18% in 2020-21; 2021-22 agri exports hit an all-time high of US$50.2 billion. Agriculture & Allied = 18.4% of India's GVA at current prices (2022-23, BHARAT 2025); accounts for ~1/7th of GDP and sustains ~2/3rds of the population.

Real GVA grew 6.2% in H1 FY25 (strong Q1, subdued Q2); agriculture and services were the key growth drivers. Agri growth: Q2 FY25 = 3.5% (best in 4 quarters), aided by healthy Kharif output, above-normal monsoon, adequate reservoir levels. First advance estimates (2024-25): Kharif foodgrain production = record 1,647.05 lakh metric tonnes (LMT), +5.7% over 2023-24, +8.2% over the 5-year average. As of 10 Jan 2025, Rabi wheat/gram sowing was up 1.4%/0.8% YoY.

Foodgrain production rose from 265.05 million tonnes (2013-14) to a record 328.85 million tonnes (2023-24); horticulture production = 352.23 million tonnes (3rd advance estimate). (Source: BHARAT 2025)

International Year of Millets — UNGA's 75th session (March 2021) declared 2023 the International Year of Millets; millets are "Smart Food" — high nutrition, climate-resilient, aligned with UN SDGs.

8PMFBY, MSP & Related Schemes — Full Detail

PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana) — launched 2016 Kharif season for comprehensive crop-failure insurance/income stabilisation. World's largest crop-insurance scheme by farmer enrolment (avg. 5.5 crore applications/year) and 3rd-largest by premium received. Farmer premium share: 1.5% (Rabi) / 2% (Kharif) of total premium — Centre+State bear the rest. Over its first 6 years, farmers paid ₹25,186 crore in premiums and received ₹1.2 lakh crore in claims (as of 31 Oct 2022).

Allied sectors (horticulture, animal husbandry, dairy, fisheries) are important for rural economic conditions/nutrition. Commercial crops = grown for trade, not self-consumption — includes oilseed, sugar, fibre, narcotic, and beverage crops. ACPC (Agriculture Cost and Price Commission, per Lucent's naming) decides MSP on the government's behalf. MSP = the price at which government commits to purchase crops directly from farmers if the market price falls below it.

9Sweet Revolution, Blue Economy 2.0 & Full Commodity Board Table

Sweet Revolution — govt approved ₹500 crore for the National Beekeeping & Honey Mission (NBHM) over 3 years (2020-21 to 2022-23), under the Atma Nirbhar Bharat (ANB) scheme; promotes scientific beekeeping via the National Bee Board (NBB).

Blue Economy 2.0 — highlighted in the Interim Budget 2024 (1 Feb 2024); emphasises sustainable development of coastal aquaculture, seas, and coasts; pillars: Mariculture, Maritime Transport, Renewable Energy, Tourism; balances environmental preservation with economic activity. World Bank's definition: "sustainable use of ocean resources for economic growth, improved livelihoods and jobs while preserving the health of the ocean ecosystem."

10Allied Sectors — Livestock, Fisheries & Dairy

Livestock, forestry/logging, and fishing/aquaculture are buoyant-growth allied sectors and a potential source of better farm incomes. Livestock sector grew at 7.9% CAGR (2014-15 to 2020-21, constant prices); its share of agriculture GVA rose from 24.3% (2014-15) to 30.1% (2020-21). Fisheries sector — ~7% annual average growth since 2016-17; ~6.7% share of agriculture GVA. Allied sectors' faster growth than crops raises their share of total agri-GVA over time.

Dairy — the most critical livestock component, employing 8+ crore farmers directly; India ranks 1st in milk production globally, 3rd in egg production, 8th in meat production.

11National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013

Objective: food/nutritional security across the human life cycle via affordable access to adequate quality food, ensuring dignity. Notified 10 September 2013; covers up to 75% of the rural population and up to 50% of the urban population for subsidised foodgrains under the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS) — about 2/3rds of the total population; special nutritional focus on women and children. (Source: MOCAFPD)

12Green Revolution, Revolutions & Land Reform — Additional Lucent Detail

Per Lucent: credit for the Green Revolution in India goes to agricultural scientist Dr. Norman Borlaug; Dr. M.S. Swaminathan's contribution is "not less" — but the "termed name" is credited to American scientist "William Gande" (a likely garbled rendering of William Gaud, who coined the term "Green Revolution" in 1968). Elsewhere the book separately calls Swaminathan the leading figure in India's Green Revolution and Borlaug "The Father of Green Revolution" in the world — an internal inconsistency, both preserved verbatim.

White Revolution = rapid rise in milk production; driven by Operation Flood, "Father": Dr. Verghese Kurien; the world's largest integrated dairy-development programme, started by the National Dairy Development Board in 1970. Yellow Revolution = rise in oilseed production. Blue Revolution = rise in fish production.

11th Plan (2007-12): agriculture's plan outlay fell to just 18.5%; average annual agri/allied GDP growth = 3.7% — short of the 4% target but better than the 10th Plan's 2.4%. The 10th Plan was the first plan not to fix crop-production targets. Green Revolution did not cover barley, ragi, or minor millets — confined to HYV rice, wheat, maize, jowar. National Agriculture Insurance Scheme implemented October 1999.

Land-holding reorganisation used two measures: Land Ceiling (max legal landholding) and Chakbandi (consolidating fragmented/divided land) — Chakbandi was first implemented in India in 1920, Baroda. Landholding classes: Marginal <1 ha, Small 1-4 ha, Large >4 ha.

Per the source's own pink-box correction: M.S. Swaminathan, Indian geneticist/international administrator, is renowned for leading India's Green Revolution, while Norman E. Borlaug is called "The Father of Green Revolution" globally. Green Revolution "started in the Third Five Year Plan" (per Lucent p.258) — its most positive effect was on wheat, with a 500% increase in crop production (as printed, unverified statistic).

13Agricultural Finance & NABARD — Full Detail

Unorganised finance sources: money-lenders, money-dealers, relatives, businessmen, landlords, commission agents. Organised sources: Co-operative Committees, Co-operative Banks, Commercial Banks, RRBs, the Government. Co-operative Credit Organisation started 1904; Primary Co-operative Committees give short-period credit; State Co-operative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks give long-period credit. Land Development Bank (originally "Land Mortgage Bank") established 1919 — long-term loans.

NABARD — apex rural-credit institution; established 12 July 1982 by merging the RBI's Agriculture Credit Department and the Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation; based on the Sivaraman Committee (printed "Shivraman") recommendations. Authorised share capital: ₹500 crore initially → increased to ₹5,000 crore, effective 1 February 2001 → a further six-fold increase to ₹30,000 crore via the NABARD (Amendment) Bill 2017 (passed January 2018), which also enabled RBI's exit from NABARD's ownership.
